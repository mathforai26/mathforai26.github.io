\noindent\textbf{About these lecture notes.}
These notes return to the problem introduced in Lecture~1: building a machine
that generates natural language. Lecture~2 studied deterministic labels. Here
we allow the same context to have different continuations and ask what a
probability model should learn from their frequencies. This leads to proper
losses, log loss, statistical efficiency, total variation, betting, and
compression.

\section{From deterministic labels to probability distributions}
\label{sec:from-labels}

Lecture~2 considered a target function $f:[N]\to\{0,1\}$. Once a box index
$x$ was fixed, its label $f(x)$ was fixed. Natural language does not have this
property. A prefix such as
\[
  \text{``The meeting begins at''}
\]
can be followed naturally by different times, phrases, or punctuation. A
language generator must represent more than one acceptable continuation.
We begin with a smaller problem than language. There are $N$ possible outcomes,
indexed by $[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}$. We identify a function
$q:[N]\to\R$ with the vector $(q(1),\ldots,q(N))$, so
$\R^{[N]}\equiv\R^N$. Under this identification, the set of probability
distributions on $[N]$ is the simplex
\begin{equation}
\Delta_N
=\left\{q\in\R^{[N]}:q(x)\ge0\text{ for every }x,
\quad \sum_{x=1}^N q(x)=1\right\}
\subseteq\R^N.
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
Let $p\in\Delta_N$ be the data distribution and suppose
\begin{equation}
X_1,\ldots,X_T\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}p.
\label{eq:iid-categorical}
\end{equation}
The empirical distribution records relative frequencies:
\begin{equation}
\widehat p_T(x)
=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\I\{X_t=x\},
\qquad x\in[N].
\label{eq:empirical-distribution}
\end{equation}
For each $x$, the law of large numbers gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat p_T(x)\longrightarrow p(x)
\qquad\text{almost surely as }T\to\infty.
\label{eq:frequency-matching}
\end{equation}
When $N$ is small and outcomes repeat, the empirical distribution solves the
frequency-matching problem directly.

The problem changes when the outcome space or the collection of contexts is
large. Many relevant events will occur rarely or not at all. We then introduce
a structured model
\begin{equation}
\{p_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\},
\label{eq:parametric-model}
\end{equation}
where one parameter vector controls probabilities at many outcomes or
contexts. A transformer reuses the same weights to produce a next-token
distribution at every context. The question is how to choose $\theta$ from
data.

For text, the categorical problem is repeated conditionally. At a prefix
$x_{<t}=(x_1,\ldots,x_{t-1})$, a language model reports
\[
p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\in\Delta_N.
\]
Multiplication gives the autoregressive sequence model
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(x_{1:T})
=\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}).
\label{eq:autoregressive-factorization}
\end{equation}
Three terms commonly used for this setup describe different aspects of the
learning problem.

\begin{definition}[Unsupervised, self-supervised, and generative modeling]
\label{def:learning-terms}
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.7em,labelindent=0pt,
  itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.3em]
\item[Unsupervised learning]
The training data are observations without separately supplied task labels.
The learner seeks regularities in the distribution of those observations.
\item[Self-supervised learning]
The prediction targets are constructed from the observations themselves. For
next-token prediction, a sequence $x_{1:T}$ supplies the input--target pairs
$(x_{<t},x_t)$; no additional annotation is required.
\item[Generative modeling]
The model specifies a probability distribution from which observations can in
principle be sampled. The factorization in
\cref{eq:autoregressive-factorization} defines such a distribution over
complete token sequences.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

Next-token language modeling is called self-supervised because the tokens
provide their own prediction targets, and generative because the fitted
conditionals define a joint distribution that can generate sequences. It has
also traditionally been called unsupervised because it requires no separately
annotated labels. The terms overlap, but they answer different questions:
self-supervised describes how targets are obtained, while generative describes
what kind of model is learned. The practice of fitting probability
models to observations has a long history in statistics
and predates this terminology by many decades.

\section{Losses that recover the probabilities}
\label{sec:proper-losses}

We still need a rule for choosing a distribution from the observations. In a
structured model, this means choosing $\theta$ and thereby choosing
$p_\theta$. A loss assigns a numerical assessment to each candidate
distribution and observed outcome; averaging these assessments gives an
objective that can be minimized over $q$, or over $q=p_\theta$.

The numerical assessment must depend on two things: the distribution we chose
and the outcome that occurred. The first is under our control; the second is
supplied by the data. The unknown data distribution $p$ cannot be an input to
the learning algorithm.

\begin{definition}[Loss for categorical probability prediction]
\label{def:probability-loss}
A loss for categorical probability prediction is a function
\begin{equation}
\ell:\Delta_N\times[N]\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}.
\label{eq:loss-definition}
\end{equation}
Its value $\ell(q,x)$ measures the loss incurred when we choose
$q\in\Delta_N$ and observe $x\in[N]$.
\end{definition}

Given observations $X_1,\ldots,X_T$, empirical risk minimization chooses
\begin{equation}
\widehat q_T
\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{q\in\Delta_N}
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\ell(q,X_t).
\label{eq:erm-distribution}
\end{equation}

Let $X$ have distribution $p$. For any fixed $q$ for which the loss has finite
expectation, the law of large numbers gives
\begin{equation}
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\ell(q,X_t)
\longrightarrow
\mathbb E[\ell(q,X)]
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
\label{eq:loss-lln}
\end{equation}

Before asking how fast \cref{eq:erm-distribution} converges, ask whether the
population objective asks for the correct distribution. With a mild abuse of
the same notation, write
\begin{equation}
\ell(q,p)
=\mathbb E[\ell(q,X)]
=\sum_{x=1}^N p(x)\ell(q,x).
\label{eq:population-loss}
\end{equation}
Thus $\ell(q,x)$ is the loss on one realized outcome, whereas $\ell(q,p)$ is
its expectation when the outcome has distribution $p$.

\begin{definition}[Proper and strictly proper loss]
\label{def:proper-loss}
A loss $\ell$ is \emph{proper} if
\[
\ell(p,p)\le \ell(q,p)
\qquad\text{for all }p,q\in\Delta_N.
\]
It is \emph{strictly proper} if equality is possible only when $q=p$.
\end{definition}

Properness says that truthful probability reporting minimizes expected loss.
Strict properness identifies the entire distribution, not only its most likely
outcome. This is the formal version of matching natural frequencies.

Two important examples are the \emph{log loss}
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\log}(q,x)=-\log q(x)
\label{eq:log-loss}
\end{equation}
and the \emph{squared probability loss}, also called the multiclass
\emph{Brier loss},
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\rm B}(q,x)
=\sum_{j=1}^N\bigl(q(j)-\I\{x=j\}\bigr)^2
=\lVert q-e_x\rVert_2^2.
\label{eq:brier-loss}
\end{equation}
Here $e_x\in\R^N$ is the vector with a one in coordinate $x$ and zeros
elsewhere.

Unless a base is displayed, $\log$ denotes the natural logarithm. Log loss
measured with the natural logarithm has units of \emph{nats}; log loss measured
with $\log_2$ has units of \emph{bits}. One nat equals
$\log_2(e)\approx1.4427$ bits. We set $-\log 0=+\infty$.

For $p,q\in\Delta_N$, define
\begin{equation}
H(p)=-\sum_{x=1}^N p(x)\log p(x),
\qquad
\KL(p, q)=\sum_{x=1}^N p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
\label{eq:entropy-kl}
\end{equation}
Terms with $p(x)=0$ are defined to be zero, including when $q(x)=0$. A term
with $p(x)>0$ and $q(x)=0$ is $+\infty$. Thus $\KL(p, q)$ is finite
exactly when $q(x)>0$ for every $x$ with $p(x)>0$. Relative entropy is not
symmetric: in general, $\KL(p, q)\ne\KL(q, p)$.\endnote{\textbf{Notation for relative entropy.}
We write this divergence as \(D(p,q)\). Common alternatives include
\(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathbin\Vert q)\), which names the divergence and visually
marks its direction, and \(\operatorname{KL}(p,q)\), which is explicit but
longer. The shorter \(D(p,q)\) is unambiguous here because no other divergence
is denoted by \(D\); the order of its two arguments records the direction.}

The conventions at zero are forced by continuity and by the operational
meaning of log loss. If \(p(x)=0\), the contribution
\(p(x)\log(p(x)/q(x))\) tends to zero as \(p(x)\downarrow0\). If
\(p(x)>0=q(x)\), the report assigns zero probability to an event that occurs,
so its expected log loss is infinite. \Cref{ex:proper-regrets} derives these
limiting statements.

The logarithm in \cref{eq:log-loss} may initially look arbitrary. The next
identity establishes that it is a valid loss for probability estimation.

\begin{proposition}[Regret identities]
\label{prop:regret-identities}
For every $p,q\in\Delta_N$,
\begin{align}
\ell_{\log}(q,p)
&=H(p)+\KL(p, q),
\label{eq:log-regret}\\
\ell_{\rm B}(q,p)
&=1-\lVert p\rVert_2^2+\lVert p-q\rVert_2^2,
\label{eq:brier-regret}
\end{align}
Consequently, log loss and Brier loss are strictly proper.
\end{proposition}

The terms $H(p)$ and $1-\lVert p\rVert_2^2$ do not depend on the report $q$.
The avoidable population losses, or \emph{regrets}, are therefore
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\log}(q,p)-\ell_{\log}(p,p)=\KL(p, q)
\label{eq:log-regret-short}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\rm B}(q,p)-\ell_{\rm B}(p,p)=\lVert p-q\rVert_2^2.
\label{eq:brier-regret-short}
\end{equation}
The exercise in \cref{ex:proper-regrets} asks for the two identities, the
nonnegativity and equality condition for KL, and the resulting strict
properness of both losses.

There are many strictly proper losses. In fact, differentiable proper losses
on a finite simplex are generated by convex functions and their regrets are
Bregman divergences; see \cref{app:bregman}. Properness alone therefore does
not select the loss used in language modeling.

\section{Why single out log loss?}
\label{sec:why-log-loss}

Statistical efficiency gives the first reason to prefer log loss. A structured
model, including a transformer, uses the same parameter to determine many
probabilities. Different losses can combine the information in the observations
differently.

\begin{definition}[Statistical efficiency, predictive form]
\label{def:statistical-efficiency}
Let $\mathcal M=\{p_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$ be a regular finite-dimensional
parametric model. For each sample size $T$, let
$\widehat\theta_T=\widehat\theta_T(X_1,\ldots,X_T)$ be an estimator. The
sequence $(\widehat\theta_T)_{T\ge1}$ is \emph{statistically efficient in
$\mathcal M$} if the following holds for every $\theta_*\in\Theta$. When
$X_1,\ldots,X_T$ are iid from $p_{\theta_*}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb E\!\left[
\KL(p_{\theta_*}, p_{\widehat\theta_T})
\right]
=\frac{c_{\mathcal M}(\theta_*)}{T}+o(T^{-1}),
\label{eq:efficiency-definition}
\end{equation}
where no regular estimator sequence has a smaller coefficient than
$c_{\mathcal M}(\theta_*)$.
\end{definition}

This definition compares the leading coefficient of the prediction error as
$T\to\infty$. It does not claim that the efficient estimator has the smallest
error at every finite sample size. The estimator sequence is fixed in advance
and must attain the smallest coefficient at every possible true parameter in
the model.

\begin{definition}[Equivalent losses]
\label{def:equivalent-losses}
Two losses are \emph{equivalent} if
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\ell(q,x)=a\ell(q,x)+b(x)
\label{eq:equivalent-losses}
\end{equation}
for every $q\in\Delta_N$ and $x\in[N]$, where $a>0$ and
$b:[N]\to\R$ do not depend on $q$.
Equivalent losses have the same empirical minimizers in every model.
\end{definition}

Given a parametric model and observations $X_1,\ldots,X_T$, statisticians call
any parameter satisfying
\begin{equation}
\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}
\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta\in\Theta}
\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(X_t)
=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}
\sum_{t=1}^T-\log p_\theta(X_t)
\label{eq:mle-definition}
\end{equation}
a \emph{maximum-likelihood estimator}, or MLE. Thus maximum likelihood is the
statistical name for empirical log-loss minimization.

\begin{theorem}[Model-agnostic efficiency of log loss]
\label{thm:model-agnostic-efficiency}
Let $N\ge2$, let $\mathcal M=\{p_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$ be a regular
parametric model on $[N]$, and let $X_1,\ldots,X_T$ be iid from
$p_{\theta_*}\in\mathcal M$. The empirical log-loss minimizer is statistically
efficient in $\mathcal M$. If $N\ge3$, then, among smooth strictly proper
categorical losses, only losses equivalent to log loss have the property that
their empirical minimizer is statistically efficient in every regular
parametric model.
\end{theorem}

The guarantee is model-agnostic because the loss is selected without knowing
which regular parametric model will be used. If $q$ is optimized directly over
$\Delta_N$, log loss and Brier loss both return the empirical distribution;
\cref{ex:empirical-frequencies} asks students to verify this. A difference can
appear when a shared parameter links several probabilities and the loss must
combine information with different noise levels. The two-context calculation
in \cref{sec:two-context-efficiency} gives an explicit example.\endnote{\textbf{The binary exception.}
The condition $N\ge3$ in the uniqueness statement is necessary for an
unconditional categorical model. A nonconstant regular model in $\Delta_2$ is
locally the full Bernoulli model, so every strictly proper loss returns the
empirical success proportion. Binary regression with multiple contexts is
different because one parameter can link several Bernoulli probabilities; the
two-context example has exactly this structure.}

The appendix in \cref{app:efficiency} states the regularity assumptions,
derives the asymptotic prediction error, and discusses the applicability of the
result to neural network models.

The definition of statistical efficiency itself measures prediction error by
KL, while the theorem concerns log-loss minimization. This can make the
conclusion appear to be built into the definition. We therefore need reasons
to care about KL that do not already assume that log loss is the preferred
loss. We begin with total variation.

\section{KL divergence and total variation}
\label{sec:kl-tv}

We first ask whether small KL guarantees agreement on probabilities and bounded
predictions. Total variation expresses exactly this form of agreement.

The terminology distinguishes the object being measured from the function
used to compare objects. The \(\ell_1\) expression below is one half of a norm
on signed measures. Restricting it to probability laws gives a
\emph{distance}, or \emph{metric}: it is nonnegative, symmetric, satisfies the
triangle inequality, and vanishes only when its arguments agree. A
\emph{pseudometric} has the same properties except that distinct objects may
have discrepancy zero. For example,
\(|\E_p[f]-\E_q[f]|\) is a pseudometric on probability laws when \(f\) is
fixed. We will use this distinction again for task-specific costs.

\begin{definition}[Total variation]
\label{def:tv}
For $p,q\in\Delta_N$, their total-variation distance is
\begin{equation}
\TV(p,q)
=\sup_{A\subseteq[N]}|p(A)-q(A)|
=\frac12\sum_{x=1}^N|p(x)-q(x)|.
\label{eq:tv-definition}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Total variation controls the probability of every event. For every event $A$,
\begin{equation}
|p(A)-q(A)|\le\TV(p,q).
\label{eq:tv-events}
\end{equation}
More generally, let $X$ have distribution $p$ and $Y$ have distribution $q$.
For every $f:[N]\to[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
|\mathbb E[f(X)]-\mathbb E[f(Y)]|\le\TV(p,q).
\label{eq:tv-bounded-functions}
\end{equation}
Thus small total variation controls the probability of every event and the
expectation of every bounded measurement simultaneously.
If a prediction is the expectation of a bounded function under the estimated
distribution, its error is therefore controlled by the total variation from the
true distribution. The equality in \cref{eq:tv-definition} and the bounds in
\cref{eq:tv-events,eq:tv-bounded-functions} are proved in
\cref{ex:pinsker}. Pinsker's inequality connects this criterion to KL.

More generally, recording a deterministic function of an outcome cannot
increase KL.
\begin{proposition}[Data processing for deterministic maps]
\label{prop:kl-data-processing}
Let \(p,q\) be laws on a finite set \(\cX\), and let
\(f:\cX\to\cY\). If \(p_f,q_f\) are the push-forward laws of \(f(X)\), then
\[
  \KL(p_f,q_f)\le \KL(p,q).
\]
\end{proposition}
The log-sum argument in \cref{ex:pinsker}(d)--(e) proves the proposition by
grouping outcomes with the same value of \(f\). Taking \(f=\I_A\) gives
\(\operatorname{kl}(p(A),q(A))\le \KL(p,q)\).

\begin{theorem}[Pinsker's inequality]
\label{thm:pinsker}
For every $p,q\in\Delta_N$,
\begin{equation}
\TV(p,q)
\le \sqrt{\frac12\KL(p, q)}.
\label{eq:pinsker}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Pinsker's inequality implies the following distributional guarantee from
excess log loss:
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\log}(q,p)-\ell_{\log}(p,p)\le\epsilon
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\TV(p,q)\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}.
\label{eq:log-to-tv}
\end{equation}
Its proof is \cref{ex:pinsker}.

This raises a sharper question: would total variation select the same efficient
estimator as KL? In the regular finite-dimensional setting, the answer is yes.

\begin{proposition}[Efficiency measured by total variation]
\label{prop:tv-efficiency}
In the setting of \cref{thm:model-agnostic-efficiency}, the empirical log-loss
minimizer has the smallest leading expected total-variation error among regular
estimators. The total-variation error is of order $T^{-1/2}$. If $N\ge3$,
requiring this property in every regular parametric model again singles out log
loss, up to equivalence, among smooth strictly proper categorical losses.
\end{proposition}

Pinsker's inequality alone does not prove this proposition because it gives
only a one-sided comparison. The local calculation is
\begin{equation}
\TV(p_{\theta_*},p_{\theta_*+h})
=\frac12\sum_{x=1}^N
\left|\nabla_\theta p_{\theta_*}(x)^\top h\right|
+o(\lVert h\rVert_2).
\label{eq:local-tv-expansion}
\end{equation}
The leading term is a norm on local changes of the distribution. The
information bound says that the maximum-likelihood estimator has the smallest
asymptotic spread among regular estimators, which also minimizes the expected
value of this norm. The appendix in \cref{app:tv-efficiency} gives the argument.

Thus the efficiency result is not an artifact of defining prediction error by
log-loss regret. KL remains especially convenient because it is smooth and its
local expansion uses Fisher information directly. The TV argument is one reason
to take KL seriously; betting and coding provide two more.

\section{Prediction as betting}
\label{sec:betting}

Betting gives a second possible justification for KL by connecting stated
probabilities to consequential predictions. In the game below, a predictor's
probabilities determine bets, and prediction quality is defined by long-run
logarithmic wealth growth. The resulting equivalence is exact. Its relevance
depends on accepting this game and this definition of successful prediction.

Suppose a market offers mutually exclusive outcomes $[N]$. Fix reference odds
$r\in\Delta_N$ with $r(x)>0$. One dollar staked on outcome $x$ returns
$1/r(x)$ dollars if $x$ occurs. A bettor
is given the option to split up their wealth and make a bet.
Specifically, if the bettor chooses $q\in \Delta_N$, this means that
fraction $q(x)$ of the bettor's current wealth will be placed on outcome $x$
(the fractions sum to one, so the bettor is required to invest all current wealth across
the possible outcomes.)
Then, a random $X\in [N]$ outcome is drawn.
If $X=x$ occurs, wealth is multiplied by
\begin{equation}
\frac{q(x)}{r(x)}.
\label{eq:wealth-multiplier}
\end{equation}
While the bettor is required to bet, if they choose the reference $r$
for $q$, no matter the outcome, their wealth will be preserved.
As such, the game is quite favorable for the bettors.

Below we consider the case when a bettor bets with the same fractions $q$
in a repeated game as described above where in round $t\in [T]$,
the outcome $X_t\in [N]$ comes
from some distribution $p\in \Delta_N$. The outcomes are independent of each
other and also from the initial wealth $W_0>0$ of the bettor.
Then, from the rules of the game,
\[
W_{t+1} = W_t \cdot \frac{q(X_{t+1})}{r(X_{t+1})}\,, \qquad t=0,1,2,\dots
\]
Let $\rho_T = \frac1T \log (W_T/W_0)$ be the growth rate of wealth after $T$ steps.
\begin{proposition}[Log loss and optimal growth]
\label{prop:kelly}
Consider $(W_t,X_t)_t$ as above and any $q\in\Delta_N$. Then
$\rho_T \to G_p(q;r)$ as $T\to \infty$ almost surely, where
\begin{equation}
G_p(q;r)
=\sum_{x=1}^N p(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{r(x)}.
\label{eq:growth-rate}
\end{equation}
The sum uses the conventions introduced for relative entropy. In particular,
$G_p(q;r)=-\infty$ if $q(x)=0$ for some $x$ with $p(x)>0$.
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
G_p(p;r)-G_p(q;r)=\KL(p, q).
\label{eq:growth-regret}
\end{equation}
Therefore truthful betting, $q=p$, uniquely maximizes the long-run growth rate.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First suppose that $q(x)>0$ whenever $p(x)>0$. After $T$ rounds,
\begin{equation}
T\rho_T =  \log\frac{W_T}{W_0}
=\sum_{t=1}^T\log\frac{q(X_t)}{r(X_t)}.
\label{eq:cumulative-wealth}
\end{equation}
Divide by $T$ and apply the law of large numbers to obtain
\cref{eq:growth-rate}.
The identity in \cref{eq:growth-regret} follows by algebra.
If $q(x)=0$ for some $x$ with $p(x)>0$, that outcome occurs eventually almost
surely. The bettor's wealth is then zero, so $\rho_T=-\infty$ from that time
onward. This proves the remaining case. The strict equality condition for KL
proves uniqueness.
\end{proof}

Within this protocol, ``predict well and make money'' means
maximizing logarithmic wealth growth under this betting protocol. Wealth
multiplies across rounds, so its logarithm adds. Expected log wealth also
penalizes losing all capital: assigning zero probability to an event that
occurs sends log wealth to $-\infty$.

The remaining question is whether one accepts both the full-investment betting
protocol and long-run log-growth maximization as a reasonable standard for
prediction. If so, KL is exactly the loss in growth caused by using the wrong
probabilities. Different betting constraints or objectives can lead to a
different criterion.

The realized difference in cumulative log loss has an immediate meaning.
For two predictors $q$ and $q'$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{t=1}^T\bigl(\ell_{\log}(q',X_t)-
\ell_{\log}(q,X_t)\bigr)
=\log\frac{W_T^q}{W_T^{q'}},
\label{eq:loss-wealth-ratio}
\end{equation}
where $W_T^q$ is the wealth after the same outcomes for a bettor who uses $q$
in every round. One unit less cumulative log loss means a factor of $e$ as much
wealth for the same outcomes.

\section{Prediction as compression}
\label{sec:compression}

Coding gives a third justification for log loss: log loss in bits is the ideal
codelength assigned to the observed data. Use logarithms to base two in this
section. An ideal code matched to $q$ assigns
approximately
\begin{equation}
-\log_2 q(x)
\label{eq:single-code-length}
\end{equation}
bits to outcome $x$.\endnote{\textbf{Integer codelengths.}
Exact prefix codes require integer lengths and satisfy the Kraft inequality;
block or arithmetic coding makes the discrepancy negligible per symbol over
a long sequence.}

If the true distribution is $p$, the expected excess ideal codelength from
using $q$ instead of $p$ is
\begin{equation}
\sum_{x=1}^N p(x)(-\log_2 q(x))
-\sum_{x=1}^N p(x)(-\log_2 p(x))
=\sum_{x=1}^N p(x)\log_2\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
\label{eq:coding-regret}
\end{equation}
This is relative entropy measured in bits. Thus KL is the expected codelength
penalty for using probabilities $q$ when the data have distribution $p$.

For an autoregressive model $Q$,
\begin{align}
-\log_2 Q(x_{1:T})
&=-\log_2\prod_{t=1}^TQ(x_t\mid x_{<t})
\nonumber\\
&=\sum_{t=1}^T-\log_2Q(x_t\mid x_{<t}).
\label{eq:sequence-code-length}
\end{align}
The average log loss in bits per token is therefore the average codelength per
token under the model. If model $Q$ improves on model $R$ by $\delta$ bits per
token on the same tokenized sequence, it saves $T\delta$ bits on that sequence:
\begin{equation}
\log_2\frac{Q(x_{1:T})}{R(x_{1:T})}
=T\delta.
\label{eq:compression-likelihood-ratio}
\end{equation}

If the measured average is $b$ bits per token, its \emph{perplexity} is
\begin{equation}
2^b
=\left(\prod_{t=1}^T\frac{1}{Q(x_t\mid x_{<t})}\right)^{1/T}.
\label{eq:perplexity}
\end{equation}
This is the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities assigned to the
realized tokens.\endnote{\textbf{Interpreting perplexity.}
Perplexity can be read as an effective branching factor when the next-token
uncertainty behaves roughly like a uniform choice among a fixed number of
alternatives. For a general nonuniform, context-dependent model, perplexity
remains the geometric mean in \cref{eq:perplexity}; it is not a literal count
of available continuations.}

The betting and coding calculations are the same likelihood-ratio identity:
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{-\log_2 r(x)}_{\text{reference codelength}}
-\underbrace{\bigl(-\log_2 q(x)\bigr)}_{\text{model codelength}}
=\underbrace{\log_2\frac{q(x)}{r(x)}}_{\text{log wealth relative to reference}}.
\label{eq:betting-coding}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The model must be counted when it is not already shared}
\label{sec:model-cost}

The formula $-\log_2Q(D)$ assumes that encoder and decoder already possess the
same model $Q$. If a compression claim concerns the data used to construct the
model, its description must also be transmitted. A two-part accounting is
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm total}(D,\theta)
=L(\theta)-\log_2Q_\theta(D),
\label{eq:two-part-code}
\end{equation}
where $L(\theta)$ is the number of bits needed to specify the model under an
agreed representation.

Without $L(\theta)$, the training-data compression problem has a trivial
zero-codelength solution. Define a lookup model $Q_D$ that assigns probability
one to the particular training text $D$. Then
\begin{equation}
-\log_2Q_D(D)=0.
\label{eq:lookup-zero}
\end{equation}
But describing $Q_D$ requires specifying which text receives probability one;
that description contains essentially the text itself. This is the precise
sense in which, if model size is ignored, ``the best compressor of the text is
the text as the model.'' No reusable compression has been demonstrated.

If a trained model is already shared and we encode new held-out text, charging
its full training cost again answers a different question. Then
$-\log_2Q_\theta(D_{\rm test})$ is a legitimate conditional codelength. State
which resources are shared before making a compression claim.

\subsection{What compression establishes}

Low log loss and good conditional compression are mathematically equivalent.
Therefore the statement ``a good language model compresses text'' follows from
the choice of log loss. If the model is already shared, a shorter codelength on
held-out data than a specified baseline is evidence that the model captures
reusable predictive regularities. This comparison does not identify which
regularities were used. They may include syntax, phrase repetition, topic,
formatting, factual associations, discourse structure, or reusable reasoning
steps.

The stronger claim that compression by itself explains intelligence requires
an additional argument: why the predictive regularities needed for compression
also support the capabilities of interest. The coding identity does not supply
that argument.

\section{Take-home messages}
\label{sec:take-home}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.3em,topsep=0.4em]
\item Deterministic labels are too narrow for language. A context can have many
natural continuations, so the target is a conditional distribution.
\item A strictly proper loss is uniquely minimized in expectation by the true
distribution. Log loss and Brier loss are both strictly proper.
\item Properness does not select one loss. Among smooth strictly proper losses,
log loss is, up to equivalence, the unique choice that guarantees statistical
efficiency across regular categorical models with $N\ge3$. Multiple coupled
binary contexts also distinguish the losses.
\item Excess log loss is KL divergence. Pinsker's inequality converts it into
control of every event probability through total variation.
\item Under a repeated betting protocol, KL is the loss in long-run log-wealth
growth caused by using the wrong probabilities.
\item Log loss in bits is codelength. A difference in cumulative log loss is a
code-length difference and a log likelihood ratio.
\item Compression of training data must count the model description. Otherwise
a model that stores the data gives an uninformative zero data-codelength claim.
\item Compression of held-out data relative to a specified baseline is evidence
that reusable predictive regularity was captured. It does not identify the
regularity or explain every capability of the model.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Optional converse.}
Let $\ell:\Delta_N^\circ\times[N]\to\R$ be finite, differentiable in its first
argument, and strictly proper when $p,q\in\Delta_N^\circ$. Define
\begin{equation}
B(p)=\ell(p,p),
\qquad
\widetilde F(p)=-B(p).
\label{eq:bayes-loss-generator}
\end{equation}
\item Prove
\[
B(p)=\inf_{q\in\Delta_N^\circ}\ell(q,p).
\]
For fixed $q$, the map $p\mapsto\ell(q,p)$ is affine. Use this fact to prove
that $B$ is concave. Then use strict propriety to prove that $B$ is strictly
concave, so $\widetilde F$ is strictly convex.
\item For fixed $q$, define $A_q(p)=-\ell(q,p)$. Prove that $A_q$ is affine
and
\[
A_q(p)\le \widetilde F(p),
\qquad
A_q(q)=\widetilde F(q).
\]
Thus $A_q$ is a supporting affine function for $\widetilde F$ at $q$.
\item Use differentiability to prove that this supporting affine function is
the tangent affine function:
\[
-\ell(q,p)
=\widetilde F(q)+
\langle\nabla\widetilde F(q),p-q\rangle.
\]
Because both sides are affine in $p$, the identity extends from
$\Delta_N^\circ$ to its affine hull. Set $p=e_x$ and conclude that
\[
\ell(q,x)
=-\widetilde F(q)-
\langle\nabla\widetilde F(q),e_x-q\rangle.
\]
\item Conclude that every finite differentiable strictly proper categorical

\[
\ell(q,p)-\ell(p,p)=D_{\widetilde F}(p,q).
\]
\end{enumerate}

themselves: Brier loss is finite on the whole simplex, and log loss has the
extended-value definition given above. A general boundary treatment uses
closed extended-valued convex functions and subgradients. Legendre functions
provide a standard framework when gradients diverge at the boundary. This
theory is beyond the scope of the exercise.

\section*{Glossary}
\phantomsection
\label{sec:glossary}

This glossary collects the notation and terms used in the lecture.

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5em,labelindent=0pt]
\item[Probability simplex]
$\Delta_N$ is the set of nonnegative functions on $[N]$ whose values sum to
one. Identifying functions on $[N]$ with vectors gives
$\Delta_N\subseteq\R^{[N]}\equiv\R^N$.
\item[Empirical distribution]
The vector of relative frequencies in a sample.
\item[Probabilistic model]
A family of distributions, often written $\{p_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$.
Shared parameters connect probabilities across outcomes or contexts.
\item[Autoregressive model]
A sequence model that factors a joint probability into conditional next-token
probabilities as in \cref{eq:autoregressive-factorization}.
\item[Unsupervised learning]
Learning from observations without separately supplied task labels.
\item[Self-supervised learning]
Learning from prediction targets constructed from the observations themselves.
In next-token prediction, the prefix is the input and its following token is
the target.
\item[Generative modeling]
Learning a probability distribution over observations. Such a model can assign
probabilities and, in principle, generate new observations by sampling.
\item[Population loss; empirical loss]
Population loss is expected loss under the data distribution. Empirical loss
is its sample average.
\item[Maximum likelihood]
The choice of model parameter that maximizes the probability assigned to the
observed sample. It is equivalently the minimizer of empirical log loss.
\item[Proper scoring loss]
A loss whose population value is minimized by the true probability
distribution. Strict properness makes that minimizer unique.
\item[Equivalent losses]
Losses related by a positive scaling and an outcome-dependent additive term,
as in \cref{eq:equivalent-losses}. They have the same empirical minimizers.
\item[Bayes loss; generalized entropy]
For a proper loss, $B(p)=\ell(p,p)=\inf_q\ell(q,p)$ is the smallest population
loss achievable when the data distribution is $p$. Under the loss convention,
$B$ is concave.
\item[Supporting affine function]
An affine function $A$ supports a convex function $F$ at $q$ if
$A(p)\le F(p)$ for every $p$ and $A(q)=F(q)$. If $F$ is differentiable, this
is its tangent affine function at $q$.
\item[Bregman divergence]
For differentiable convex $F$,
$D_F(p,q)=F(p)-F(q)-\langle\nabla F(q),p-q\rangle$. It is the gap between
$F(p)$ and the tangent affine approximation to $F$ made at $q$.
\item[Savage representation]
The characterization of proper categorical losses by convex functions and
their supporting affine functions. In the differentiable case, the associated
regrets are Bregman divergences.
\item[Log loss; cross-entropy]
The realized loss $-\log q(x)$. Its population value is cross-entropy and its
regret is $\KL(p, q)$.
\item[Brier loss]
Squared Euclidean distance between the reported probability vector and the
one-hot realized outcome. Its regret is $\lVert p-q\rVert_2^2$.
\item[Entropy]
$H(p)=-\sum_xp(x)\log p(x)$, the minimum population log loss under $p$.
\item[KL divergence]
$\KL(p, q)=\sum_xp(x)\log(p(x)/q(x))$. It is nonnegative, asymmetric, and
infinite if $q$ assigns zero probability to an event of positive
$p$-probability. A term with $p(x)=0$ contributes zero.
\item[Binary KL divergence]
$\operatorname{kl}(a,b)$ is the KL divergence from
$\operatorname{Bernoulli}(a)$ to $\operatorname{Bernoulli}(b)$, as defined in
\cref{eq:binary-kl}.
\item[Total variation]
The largest discrepancy between probabilities assigned to the same event by
two distributions; on a finite space it is one half of their $\ell_1$
distance.
\item[Statistical efficiency]
Attainment of the smallest leading asymptotic estimation or prediction error
within a specified regular model and class of estimators. These notes use
predictive KL as the error measure.
\item[Model-agnostic efficiency]
An efficiency guarantee that holds across regular parametric models without
adapting the loss to the particular model.
\item[Log-growth betting]
A repeated betting criterion that maximizes the long-run exponential growth
rate of wealth. Its regret under the stated protocol is KL divergence.
\item[Bits and nats]
Log loss uses bits with logarithm base two and nats with the natural logarithm.
One nat is $\log_2(e)\approx1.4427$ bits; one bit is
$\log(2)\approx0.6931$ nats.
\item[Perplexity]
The exponential of average log loss: $2^b$ when the average is $b$ bits per
token, or $e^n$ when it is $n$ nats per token. It is the geometric mean of the
inverse probabilities assigned to the realized tokens.
\item[Conditional codelength]
The number of bits needed to encode data when a probability model is already
shared. For a sequence, it is approximately $-\log_2Q(D)$.
\item[Two-part code]
A code that transmits both a model description and data encoded under that
model. Its total length has the form $L(\theta)-\log_2Q_\theta(D)$.
\end{description}

\section{Bregman divergences and proper losses}
\label{app:bregman}

This appendix records the general construction mentioned in class. For a
differentiable convex function $F:\Delta_N\to\R$, define its Bregman
divergence by
\begin{equation}
D_F(p,q)
=F(p)-F(q)-\langle\nabla F(q),p-q\rangle.
\label{eq:bregman-divergence}
\end{equation}
Convexity gives $D_F(p,q)\ge0$; strict convexity gives equality only for
$p=q$. Define
\begin{equation}
\ell_F(q,x)
=-F(q)-\langle\nabla F(q),e_x-q\rangle.
\label{eq:bregman-score}
\end{equation}
Taking expectation under $p$ gives
\begin{equation}
\ell_F(q,p)-\ell_F(p,p)=D_F(p,q).
\label{eq:bregman-regret}
\end{equation}
Hence every strictly convex differentiable $F$ produces a strictly proper
loss. The optional converse in \cref{ex:bregman-proper-loss} proves that every
finite differentiable strictly proper categorical loss on
$\Delta_N^\circ$ has this form. Nondifferentiable losses require supporting
subgradients in place of $\nabla F$, and the boundary requires the appropriate
extended-value conventions.

For
\[
F_{\log}(q)=\sum_xq(x)\log q(x),
\]
\cref{eq:bregman-score} gives $-\log q(x)$ and
$D_{F_{\log}}(p,q)=\KL(p, q)$. For
\[
F_{\rm B}(q)=\lVert q\rVert_2^2,
\]
it gives $\lVert q\rVert_2^2-2q(x)$, which differs from
$\lVert q-e_x\rVert_2^2$ only by the constant one.

This representation explains why properness does not determine a unique
loss. The generator $F$ chooses the geometry of probability error.

\section{Statistical efficiency: assumptions, example, and neural networks}
\label{app:efficiency}

This appendix explains the efficiency theorem in three steps. The regular
likelihood calculation gives the optimal first-order prediction error. A
two-context example shows that the gap between log loss and Brier loss can be
arbitrarily large. The final subsection identifies which parts of the argument
apply directly to neural networks and which parts require a fixed-dimensional
regular model.

\subsection{The regular likelihood result}

The regular likelihood calculation gives expected predictive KL error
$d/(2T)+o(T^{-1})$ for maximum likelihood and explains why no regular estimator
has a smaller leading term.

Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_T$ be iid from $P_{\theta_*}$ in a model
$\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\R^d\}$. We use the standard assumptions
that $\theta_*$ is an interior identifiable parameter, the log density is
sufficiently differentiable near $\theta_*$, differentiation and expectation
can be interchanged, the Fisher information is finite and nonsingular, and the
likelihood estimator is consistent. Exact assumptions vary among versions of
the theorem.

For $Z$ distributed according to $P_\theta$, define the likelihood score and
Fisher information by
\begin{equation}
s_\theta(Z)=\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(Z),
\qquad
I(\theta)=\mathbb E[s_\theta(Z)s_\theta(Z)^\top].
\label{eq:score-fisher}
\end{equation}
The score $s_\theta(Z)\in\R^d$ is a column vector. The matrix
$I(\theta)\in\R^{d\times d}$ has parameter coordinates along both axes.
Under ``mild'' regularity conditions,
\begin{equation}
\sqrt T(\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}-\theta_*)
\Longrightarrow
\mathcal N(0,I(\theta_*)^{-1}).
\label{eq:mle-asymptotic-normality}
\end{equation}
The local KL expansion is
\begin{equation}
\KL(P_{\theta_*}, P_{\theta_*+h})
=\frac12h^\top I(\theta_*)h+o(\lVert h\rVert_2^2).
\label{eq:local-kl-fisher}
\end{equation}
Combining \cref{eq:mle-asymptotic-normality} and
\cref{eq:local-kl-fisher} gives
\begin{equation}
2T\KL(P_{\theta_*}, P_{\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}})
\Longrightarrow\chi_d^2.
\label{eq:predictive-kl-chi-square}
\end{equation}
With the additional uniform-integrability conditions needed to pass to
expectations,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb E\!\left[
\KL(P_{\theta_*}, P_{\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}})
\right]
=\frac{d}{2T}+o(T^{-1}).
\label{eq:predictive-kl-rate}
\end{equation}
The results in \cref{eq:predictive-kl-chi-square,eq:predictive-kl-rate} give a
leading prediction-error rate, its
constant, and an approximate sampling distribution for confidence regions.
An efficiency ratio also approximates the sample-size ratio needed to reach
the same local accuracy.

Among regular estimators, the information bound makes
$I(\theta_*)^{-1}$ the smallest possible asymptotic covariance. If another
loss has estimating function $\psi_{\theta_*}(Z)$, equality requires
\begin{equation}
\psi_{\theta_*}(Z)=M s_{\theta_*}(Z)
\quad\text{almost surely}
\label{eq:efficiency-equality}
\end{equation}
for a nonsingular matrix $M\in\R^{d\times d}$ whose rows and columns index
parameter coordinates. Log loss has
$\psi_\theta=-s_\theta$ in every model. A proper loss that is not equivalent
to log loss can satisfy \cref{eq:efficiency-equality} in a particular model.
It lacks this guarantee uniformly across models.

\subsection{Why total variation gives the same first-order conclusion}
\label{app:tv-efficiency}

Total variation and KL have different local scales, but they select the same
first-order efficient estimator in a regular finite categorical model. For a
local parameter change $h$, define
\begin{equation}
g_{\theta_*}(h)
=\frac12\sum_{x=1}^N
\left|\nabla_\theta p_{\theta_*}(x)^\top h\right|.
\label{eq:local-tv-norm}
\end{equation}
Differentiability gives
\begin{equation}
\TV(p_{\theta_*},p_{\theta_*+h})
=g_{\theta_*}(h)+o(\lVert h\rVert_2).
\label{eq:local-tv-appendix}
\end{equation}
If
$\sqrt T(\widehat\theta_T-\theta_*)\Longrightarrow Z$, then, under the
corresponding uniform-integrability condition,
\begin{equation}
\sqrt T\,\mathbb E\!\left[
\TV(p_{\theta_*},p_{\widehat\theta_T})
\right]
\longrightarrow \mathbb E[g_{\theta_*}(Z)].
\label{eq:expected-local-tv}
\end{equation}
The convolution theorem for regular estimators says that their limiting
fluctuation equals the efficient Gaussian fluctuation plus independent
additional noise. Since
$g_{\theta_*}$ is a convex, symmetric norm on identifiable local changes,
additional noise cannot reduce its expected value. The maximum-likelihood
estimator therefore minimizes the leading expected total-variation error.

A minimum-loss estimator ties this bound only when it has the same first-order
influence function as maximum likelihood. Thus the equality condition in
\cref{eq:efficiency-equality} yields the same model-specific ties and the same
model-agnostic uniqueness conclusion as predictive KL when $N\ge3$. The
difference is the rate: expected local KL is of order $T^{-1}$, while expected
local total variation is of order $T^{-1/2}$.

\subsection{A two-context calculation}
\label{sec:two-context-efficiency}

This example shows the practical content of efficiency. Log loss combines two
estimates using their inverse variances; Brier loss weights them equally. The
resulting variance ratio can be arbitrarily large.

Let half the observations have context $a$ and half have context $b$, with
\begin{equation}
P_\theta(Y=1\mid X=a)=\theta,
\qquad
P_\theta(Y=1\mid X=b)=\frac12+\theta,
\qquad 0<\theta<\frac12.
\label{eq:two-context-model}
\end{equation}
The two empirical success proportions give unbiased estimates
\begin{equation}
\widehat\theta_a=\widehat p_a,
\qquad
\widehat\theta_b=\widehat p_b-\frac12.
\label{eq:two-group-estimates}
\end{equation}
At the true parameter, write
\begin{equation}
v_a=\theta_*(1-\theta_*),
\qquad
v_b=\frac14-\theta_*^2.
\label{eq:two-group-variances}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_a)=\frac{2v_a}{T},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_b)=\frac{2v_b}{T}.
\label{eq:two-group-estimator-variances}
\end{equation}

Brier minimization weights the two probability residuals equally:
\begin{equation}
\widehat\theta_{\rm B}
=\frac{\widehat\theta_a+\widehat\theta_b}{2},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_{\rm B})
=\frac{v_a+v_b}{2T}.
\label{eq:brier-estimator}
\end{equation}
The minimum-variance unbiased linear combination instead uses inverse-variance
weights:
\begin{equation}
\widehat\theta_{\rm oracle}
=\frac{\widehat\theta_a/v_a+\widehat\theta_b/v_b}
{1/v_a+1/v_b},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_{\rm oracle})
=\frac1T\frac{1}{\tfrac12(1/v_a+1/v_b)}.
\label{eq:oracle-estimator}
\end{equation}
It is an oracle because $v_a$ and $v_b$ depend on the unknown parameter.

The likelihood equation is
\begin{equation}
0=
\frac{\widehat\theta_a-\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}}
{v_a(\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE})}
+\frac{\widehat\theta_b-\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE}}
{v_b(\widehat\theta_{\rm MLE})},
\label{eq:mle-fixed-point}
\end{equation}
where $v_a(\theta)=\theta(1-\theta)$ and
$v_b(\theta)=1/4-\theta^2$. Thus the MLE is a fixed point of the
inverse-variance combination, using variances at its fitted value. Consistency
replaces those fitted variances by $v_a$ and $v_b$ to first order.

The asymptotic variance ratio is
\begin{equation}
\frac{V_{\rm B}}{V_{\log}}
=\frac{(v_a+v_b)^2}{4v_av_b}\ge1.
\label{eq:efficiency-ratio}
\end{equation}
As $\theta_*\downarrow0$, this ratio is asymptotic to
$1/(16\theta_*)$ and can be arbitrarily large. For every fixed
$\theta_*>0$ the model is regular, but seeing the large-sample behavior near
zero requires enough observations of the rare outcome; in particular,
$T\theta_*$ should be large.

The parameter comparison has a direct predictive meaning here. Both
conditional probabilities move one-for-one with $\theta$, so averaging
equally over the two contexts gives
\begin{equation}
\frac12\sum_{x\in\{a,b\}}
\bigl(p_{\widehat\theta}(1\mid x)-p_{\theta_*}(1\mid x)\bigr)^2
=(\widehat\theta-\theta_*)^2.
\label{eq:parameter-is-prediction}
\end{equation}
The variance ratio is therefore also the leading squared-probability-error
ratio. The example shows why structure matters: two contexts estimate the same
unknown quantity, and likelihood uses their different noise levels.

\subsection{Logistic regression and neural networks}
\label{sec:efficiency-neural-networks}

The gradient comparison applies exactly to logistic regression and neural
networks. The fixed-dimensional efficiency theorem requires additional
assumptions that overparameterized neural networks generally do not satisfy.

For binary logistic prediction, let
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(x)=\sigma(f_\theta(x)),
\qquad
g_\theta(x)=\nabla_\theta f_\theta(x).
\label{eq:binary-logistic-model}
\end{equation}
The log-loss and Brier gradients are
\begin{align}
\nabla_\theta\ell_{\log}
&=(p_\theta(x)-y)g_\theta(x),
\label{eq:binary-log-gradient}\\
\nabla_\theta\ell_{\rm B}
&=4p_\theta(x)(1-p_\theta(x))
(p_\theta(x)-y)g_\theta(x).
\label{eq:binary-brier-gradient}
\end{align}
Brier retains an additional Bernoulli-variance factor. It can suppress the
contribution of a context at which the model is saturated, including a
confidently wrong observation.

For a $K$-class neural network, let
\begin{equation}
z_\theta(x)\in\R^K,
\qquad
q_\theta(\cdot\mid x)=\operatorname{softmax}(z_\theta(x)).
\label{eq:logits-softmax}
\end{equation}
The vector $z_\theta(x)$ contains the logits. Define
\begin{equation}
J_x=\frac{\partial z_\theta(x)}{\partial\theta^\top}
\in\R^{K\times d},
\qquad
C_q=\operatorname{diag}(q)-qq^\top\in\R^{K\times K}.
\label{eq:jacobian-categorical-covariance}
\end{equation}
The rows of $J_x$ index logits and its columns index parameter coordinates.
The rows and columns of $C_q$ index classes; $C_q$ is both the softmax Jacobian
and the covariance matrix of a categorical outcome with probabilities $q$.
The gradients and the context's Fisher-information contribution are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta\ell_{\log}
&=J_x^\top(q-e_y),\\
\nabla_\theta\ell_{\rm B}
&=2J_x^\top C_q(q-e_y),\\
I_x(\theta)&=J_x^\top C_qJ_x.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:neural-gradients}
\end{equation}
The matrix $I_x(\theta)\in\R^{d\times d}$ has parameter coordinates along both
axes.
Thus $J_x$ determines which shared directions the context informs. Brier
inserts an additional $C_q$ and can suppress low-variance probability
directions.

The gradient identities above apply exactly to neural networks. The
fixed-dimensional efficiency theorem does not generally apply to
overparameterized networks, which can have nonidentifiable parameters,
singular Fisher information, growing dimension, and many interpolating
solutions. A language model still reuses its parameters across a vast number
of contexts; overparameterization relative to a finite training sample does
not make all conditional distributions independently adjustable.
The additional $C_q$ weighting may therefore matter for language models, but the
regular theorem does not establish its effect on their generalization. A
theory for that regime must account for representation, optimization,
regularization, and function-space rather than raw parameter-space behavior.

\section*{Bibliographical notes}
\phantomsection
\label{sec:bibliographical-notes}

The following sources provide the background for the results stated here.

\paragraph{Proper scoring rules.}
\citet{gneiting2007} develop proper scoring rules, entropy functions, Bregman divergences, and
examples including logarithmic, quadratic, spherical, ranked-probability, and
energy scores. Theorems~1 and~2 give the general and finite-categorical
representations in terms of convex functions and supporting affine functions.
The optional converse in \cref{ex:bregman-proper-loss} is the differentiable
finite-simplex version. Nondifferentiable scores require subgradients, and
scores such as log loss require care at the boundary. Our convention treats
smaller losses as better; the paper uses rewards, with larger values
preferred.

\paragraph{Inference from proper scores.}
\citet{dawid2016} develop estimating equations, asymptotic covariance, and efficiency for
estimators obtained from proper scoring rules. Maximum likelihood is the log
score case. The equality between an efficient estimating function and a
nonsingular transform of the likelihood score is the basis for
\cref{eq:efficiency-equality}. The regular likelihood expansions and the
convolution theorem used in \cref{app:efficiency} are treated by
\citet[Chapters~7--8]{vandervaart1998}.

\paragraph{Betting.}
\citet{kelly1956} connects information rate with exponential capital growth
under repeated betting. The protocol in \cref{sec:betting} is the
finite-outcome form needed here.

\paragraph{Compression.}
\citet{shannon1948} established the fundamental connection among probability,
entropy, and codelength. The ideal length $-\log_2q(x)$ may be nonintegral;
prefix coding, block coding, and arithmetic coding make the operational
statement precise in different ways. \citet[Chapter~14]{lattimore2020} give a
short treatment of prefix-free codes, entropy bounds, arithmetic coding, and
KL as the expected extra codelength caused by using the wrong distribution.

\paragraph{Alternative scores for language generation.}
\citet{shao2024} study training language generators with Brier and spherical
scores. Their results show that log loss is not the only trainable proper
objective. The efficiency result identifies one general statistical property
of log loss; it is not a claim that every finite-sample or misspecified
comparison favors log loss.
