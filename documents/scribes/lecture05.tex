\noindent\textbf{About these lecture notes.}
This lecture asks how a structured hidden state can explain a sequence,
how to predict and generate after observing a prefix, and how to fit the
model from observations. The main construction first fits observed lag
coefficients, then recovers a compact recurrent predictor by matrix
factorization. Fitting a dynamical model from data is called
\emph{system identification}.

For language, a latent state has a direct modeling interpretation.
Spoken or written language can be treated as a partial and noisy observation
of an evolving internal process: a speaker can form or retain thoughts that
are not expressed, and both the utterance and its reception can affect what
happens next. This is a motivating hypothesis, not a claim that the models in
this lecture describe human cognition faithfully. At a finer resolution,
motor commands, articulation, acoustics, and perception introduce further
latent variables; a state model chooses which distinctions to represent.
This explains why latent variables are natural for language, while leaving
open whether explicitly fitting them improves prediction. A transformer also
computes hidden representations, but its conditional likelihood does not
usually marginalize over a stochastic latent trajectory. An observable
conditional model can therefore be easier to train and more effective even
when a latent-process description is scientifically plausible.

\section{State, observations, and the modeling choice}
\label{sec:state-space}

A state-space model specifies what information a process retains and how
that information produces observations. Let \(\cS\) and \(\cZ\) be the
state and observation spaces. The state is \emph{latent} when it is not
directly observed. We use the equations
\begin{equation}
 S_0\sim\pi_\theta,\qquad
 S_{t+1}=f_\theta(S_t,V_{t+1}),\qquad
 Z_{t+1}=g_\theta(S_{t+1},W_{t+1}),\quad t\ge0.
 \label{eq:nonlinear-ssm}
\end{equation}
Here \((V_t)_{t\ge1}\) is state noise and \((W_t)_{t\ge1}\) is observation
noise. Each sequence is independent and identically distributed (iid);
the two sequences and \(S_0\) are mutually independent. Their laws are part of the model
and may depend on \(\theta\).
We assume measurable update maps; Euclidean spaces and finite sets cover
our examples.\endnote{\textbf{General spaces.}
The conditional distributions used here exist for finite and Euclidean
spaces. More general measurable spaces need additional regularity
assumptions.}
The notation suppresses observed, time-varying inputs from the environment.
They can enter the state update as
\(S_{t+1}=f_\theta(S_t,U_t,V_{t+1})\), and can also enter the observation
map.\endnote{\textbf{Input--output models.}
Adding an observed input \(U_t\) is a small change to the model definition,
and filtering can condition on its realized values. Learning can be more
difficult when inputs are chosen in response to past outputs or are correlated
with unobserved disturbances; causal interpretation then requires assumptions
beyond the autonomous model used here. Section~\ref{sec:rnn} returns to inputs
and feedback for recurrent and linear models.}

The process \((S_t)\) is Markov: conditional on its current state, its
next state does not depend on earlier states or observations.
We call the full construction a \emph{hidden Markov model} (HMM), and
\((Z_t)\) its \emph{observation process}, or a \emph{hidden Markov process}.
The pair process \((S_t,Z_t)_{t\ge1}\) is Markov. The observation process
alone generally is not. We will say \emph{finite HMM} when \(\cS\) is
finite. This terminology also allows a continuous-state HMM.

\subsection{Where the inductive bias enters}

The useful assumption is a small or structured state space and a
restricted update rule. As in Lecture~1, an architecture relates
predictions at different inputs through shared structure.
For physical motion, a few state coordinates can have known meanings.
For language, identifying a useful compact state is itself a learning
problem.

One structural choice divides the state into components intended to
operate at different time scales. A slower component can influence an
intermediate component, which can influence a faster component, while all
three contribute to the observation.

The dependence pattern alone does not make one component slower than
another. That distinction requires further restrictions on rates of change,
such as different update frequencies, persistence constraints, or noise
scales. When these restrictions match the process, they can provide a useful
inductive bias. The combined collection of components is still one state.
The displayed observation map uses only the fast component. Allowing it to
use all three components is a less restrictive alternative.

The representation is a modeling choice. Removing process noise gives
\[
 S_{t+1}=f_\theta(S_t),\qquad Z_t=g_\theta(S_t,W_t).
\]
All state randomness then comes from the initial state. Deterministic
dynamics can still describe elaborate processes. Even a scalar linear
state update can reproduce any binary process if the random initial
state encodes the whole sequence and a nonlinear observation map reads
successive digits.\endnote{\textbf{A linear update with a nonlinear readout.}
Let \((B_t)_{t\ge1}\) have any joint law on binary sequences. Set
\(S_0=\sum_{k\ge1}3^{-k}B_k\), \(S_{t+1}=3S_t\), and
\(Z_t=\lfloor S_t\rfloor\bmod 3\) for \(t\ge1\).
The fractional tail of \(S_t\) is at most \(1/2\), and its integer
part is \(\sum_{k=1}^t3^{t-k}B_k\), so \(Z_t=B_t\).
The initial law encodes the entire process. Exact generation for
arbitrarily long times uses arbitrarily many digits of the initial state.}
If the state and observation maps are both linear and the state is
finite-dimensional, noiseless observations instead satisfy a finite
recurrence with scalar coefficients; see \cref{sec:cayley-hamilton}.

Another choice is an \emph{observation-driven model}:
\begin{equation}
 R_{t+1}=h_\theta(R_t,Z_t),\qquad
 Z_t=j_\theta(R_t,W_t),\qquad R_1\sim\nu_\theta.
 \label{eq:observation-driven}
\end{equation}
Here \(R_1\) is independent of the iid observation noises \((W_t)\).
For fixed parameters and a specified initial state, the observed prefix
determines \(R_t\) by successive applications of \(h_\theta\).
If the initial state is random and hidden, uncertainty about it may remain.
During generation, random observations feed randomness into future states.

Every process on a finite or Euclidean observation space has an
observation-driven representation: take \(R_t=Z_{<t}\), let
\(h_\theta\) append the new observation, and let \(j_\theta\) sample
the desired conditional law using fresh uniform noise. The empty history
is a fixed initial state, and the history's length records time.
Inductive bias comes from restrictions such as a fixed state dimension
and linear maps or a prescribed neural-network architecture.
\Cref{sec:filtering} gives another representation whose state is a
conditional distribution. For linear Gaussian models this leads to the
innovations representation in \cref{sec:innovations-generation};
\emph{innovations} will mean the one-step prediction errors.

Viewed from outside the dashed enclosure, observation feedback and fresh
noise appear to produce the next observation, as in a first-order
autoregressive process. The enclosure, however, contains a delay that
stores \(R_t\). The individual function boxes are static maps; their
enclosure is a computation with memory. Substituting the state update gives
\[
 Z_{t+1}=j_\theta\bigl(h_\theta(R_t,Z_t),W_{t+1}\bigr).
\]
The state \(R_t\) remains: \(Z_t\) and fresh noise alone need not suffice
to generate \(Z_{t+1}\) with its conditional law given the full past.
The pair \((R_t,Z_t)\) is Markov, while the observation process need not
be. \Cref{ex:l5-feedback} gives an explicit counterexample.

\subsection{The equations define a probability law}
\label{sec:model-law}

The initial law, noise laws, and maps together determine a unique law
\(Q_\theta\) on state and observation sequences. A \emph{probability kernel}
assigns a probability distribution to each input state; the probability
of any fixed measurable event must depend measurably on that state.
The \emph{transition kernel} is the conditional law of the next state.
The \emph{observation kernel}, also called the \emph{emission kernel},
is the conditional law of an observation given its current state.
For measurable \(B\subseteq\cS\) and \(D\subseteq\cZ\), write them as
\[
 a_\theta(s,B)=\P(f_\theta(s,V_1)\in B),\qquad
 b_\theta(s,D)=\P(g_\theta(s,W_1)\in D).
\]
Their joint law has the product form
\begin{equation}
 Q_\theta(ds_{0:T},dz_{1:T})
 =\pi_\theta(ds_0)\prod_{t=1}^T
 a_\theta(s_{t-1},ds_t)b_\theta(s_t,dz_t).
 \label{eq:ssm-joint}
\end{equation}
For discrete variables, the kernels give conditional probability masses.
When densities exist, they are taken relative to a specified measure,
such as Lebesgue measure for real vectors. We write the corresponding
masses or densities as \(a_\theta(s'\mid s)\) and \(b_\theta(z\mid s)\).

The correspondence is not one-to-one. Different randomized maps can
implement the same kernels; different hidden representations can give
the same observation law.

For a finite HMM, let \(S_t\in[d]\) and \(X_t\in[N]\), with
\[
 A_{ij}=Q_\theta(S_t=i\mid S_{t-1}=j),\qquad
 C_{aj}=Q_\theta(X_t=a\mid S_t=j).
\]
Both matrices are \emph{column-stochastic}: their entries are nonnegative
and every column sums to one. This convention matches column-vector probability
distributions. With \(\pi_j=Q_\theta(S_0=j)\),
\begin{equation}
 Q_\theta(s_{0:T},x_{1:T})
 =\pi_{s_0}\prod_{t=1}^T A_{s_ts_{t-1}}C_{x_ts_t}.
 \label{eq:hmm-joint}
\end{equation}
Independent uniform random variables and inverse cumulative sums of the
columns implement \(f_\theta,g_\theta\); \cref{ex:l5-kernels} asks for
the construction. Here \(X_t\) is the discrete observation label;
later \(Z_t=e_{X_t}\) is its \emph{one-hot encoding}, where \(e_a\) has
entry one in position \(a\) and zero in every other position.

\section{Generation after an observed prefix}
\label{sec:filtering}

Unconditional generation follows the equations: sample \(S_0\), then
sample fresh noises at each step. Conditional generation must first
account for what the observations reveal about the hidden state.

Let
\[
 \mu_t^-=Q_\theta(S_t\in\cdot\mid Z_{<t}=z_{<t})
\]
be the \emph{predictive state distribution}. To sample a continuation
\(Z_{t:T}\), first sample \(S_t\sim\mu_t^-\), then draw fresh observation
noise \(W_t\) and set \(Z_t=g_\theta(S_t,W_t)\).
Continue with \(S_{t+1}=f_\theta(S_t,V_{t+1})\) and the remaining
observations, using fresh independent noises at each step.

A \emph{posterior} is a conditional distribution after incorporating
observed data. After seeing \(z_t\), the \emph{filtering distribution} is
\(\mu_t=Q_\theta(S_t\in\cdot\mid Z_{1:t}=z_{1:t})\).
Bayes' rule gives it and the next prediction:
\begin{equation}
 \mu_t(ds)=\frac{b_\theta(z_t\mid s)\mu_t^-(ds)}
 {\int b_\theta(z_t\mid u)\mu_t^-(du)},\qquad
 \mu_{t+1}^-(B)=\int a_\theta(s,B)\mu_t(ds).
 \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
The first formula assumes an observation density or mass and a positive
denominator. Conditional laws are specified only almost surely.

For a finite HMM, let \(\alpha_t(i)=Q_\theta(S_t=i\mid X_{1:t}=x_{1:t})\)
and \(\alpha_t^-(i)=Q_\theta(S_t=i\mid X_{<t}=x_{<t})\).
Then \(\alpha_0=\pi\) and
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_t^-=A\alpha_{t-1},\qquad
 \alpha_t=\frac{\diag(C_{x_t,1},\ldots,C_{x_t,d})\alpha_t^-}
 {\mathbf1^\top\diag(C_{x_t,1},\ldots,C_{x_t,d})\alpha_t^-}.
 \label{eq:hmm-filter}
\end{equation}
Each step costs \(O(d^2)\) for dense \(A\). This is the forward
filtering recursion. Viterbi instead finds a most likely complete
hidden path; smoothing computes posterior state distributions using
observations on both sides of the time in question.

For continuous nonlinear models, \emph{particle filtering} approximates
the filtering distribution by weighted sampled states: propagate particles
through the transition, weight them by observation likelihood, and
resample when needed, drawing replacement particles with probabilities
proportional to their weights. This algorithm uses evaluable observation
likelihoods.
\emph{Filter forgetting} means that the effect of the initial filtering
distribution diminishes as more observations arrive. It can help keep
approximation errors bounded over time.
Observation informativeness, dependence across time, dimension, and the particle count
also matter; the bibliographical notes give precise sources.
A linear Gaussian model permits exact filtering by updating means and
covariances; this is \emph{Kalman filtering}.

Filtering also gives an observation-driven representation. Take
\(R_t=\mu_t^-\), the entire predictive state distribution.
\Cref{eq:filter} updates it deterministically after observing \(z_t\);
the next observation is sampled by first drawing a state from this
distribution and then applying the observation map. The initial
\(R_1\) is the fixed law of \(S_1\), even when \(S_1\) is random.
For a finite HMM, this state is a probability vector; for a Gaussian LDS,
it is a mean and covariance. In general, it is a whole probability law,
whose update may require difficult integration.

\section{What can be learned from observations?}
\label{sec:estimation}

If finite hidden states were observed, transition and emission counts
would give direct estimates of \(A,C\). For linear models with observed
states, least squares estimates the linear maps under standard rank
conditions. Hidden states remove these directly observed targets.

We focus on a single observed trajectory \(Z_{1:T}\). With independent
trajectories, log likelihoods add and the recursions restart at each
initial state. These are straightforward algorithmic changes; the
statistical information can be substantially different.

We use \emph{mixing} here to mean decreasing statistical dependence
between observation blocks as their time separation grows; quantitative
conditions will be introduced in Lecture~6.
For example, a long mixing trajectory contains repeated information
about transitions but only one draw from its initial law. Even observing
\(S_0\) exactly would not consistently estimate an unrestricted
\(\pi_\theta\) from that one draw. Later observations do not supply
independent initial states. Mixing and \emph{excitation}---variation in
the observations that distinguishes different parameter values---help estimate the
dynamics.
Lecture~6 returns to learning from dependent data.

\subsection{Identifiability and the meaning of success}
\label{sec:identification}

A parameter is \emph{identifiable} if distinct values give distinct
observation laws. Relabeling hidden states leaves a finite HMM's observation law unchanged.
Unreachable extra states, or suitably duplicated states with identical
observable behavior, can leave it unchanged too. Thus neither the state
labels nor an unrestricted state count are identifiable.

An invertible change of hidden-state coordinates also preserves the
observation law when the update and observation maps are transformed
accordingly; \cref{sec:lds-model} gives the linear case. Beyond this coordinate
ambiguity, an observation law need not uniquely separate physical process
noise from measurement noise.

Three useful questions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=.2em]
\item How accurately does the model predict the next observation?
\item How close are its conditional or joint observation laws, for example
in KL, and its generated task statistics?
\item If the model uses the smallest state dimension needed for its
predictions, how accurately can its update and observation matrices be
recovered, allowing an invertible change of state coordinates?
\end{enumerate}
The third can help answer the first two, but the conversion needs
assumptions about sensitivity to data errors and about long-run error
propagation.

\subsection{Likelihood evaluation and optimization}
\label{sec:latent-likelihood}

The \emph{observed likelihood} is the mass or density assigned to the
observed sequence. It \emph{marginalizes} the hidden trajectory: sums or
integrates the joint law over the unobserved states. For the
finite HMM,
\begin{equation}
 Q_\theta(X_{1:T})=
 \sum_{s_{0:T}\in[d]^{T+1}}
 \pi_{s_0}\prod_{t=1}^T A_{s_ts_{t-1}}C_{X_ts_t}.
 \label{eq:hmm-likelihood}
\end{equation}
There are \(d^{T+1}\) terms with this initialization convention.
If the initial distribution is specified directly for \(S_1\),
there are \(d^T\) terms. The forward recursion evaluates the sum in
\(O(Td^2)\) arithmetic operations; normalization at each step or a
log-domain calculation prevents numerical underflow.
For continuous states, marginalization is an integral.

We fit \(\theta\) by minimizing \(-\log Q_\theta(Z_{1:T})\).
Differentiating the finite-HMM forward algorithm is feasible and is
implemented in modern software. Reverse-mode differentiation propagates
derivatives backward through the likelihood computation. It has
the same order of arithmetic work as likelihood evaluation, with
additional storage or recomputation. The parameter optimization is
generally nonconvex.

\emph{Monte Carlo} methods estimate expectations and integrals with random
samples. They are also used for likelihoods and gradients in nonlinear
state-space models. \emph{Sequential Monte Carlo} methods, including the
particle filter above, update these samples as observations arrive.

\subsection{EM and optimization over hidden trajectories}
\label{sec:em}

\emph{Expectation--maximization} (EM) uses the current parameters
\(\theta^{(k)}\) to compute a posterior over hidden trajectories,
then maximizes the expected \emph{complete-data log likelihood}, the log
joint mass or density of states and observations. The posterior expectation
is the E-step; maximizing over parameters is the M-step:
\begin{align}
 q_k(ds)&=Q_{\theta^{(k)}}(ds_{0:T}\mid Z_{1:T}),\nonumber\\
 \theta^{(k+1)}&\in\arg\max_\theta
 \E_{S\sim q_k}[\log Q_\theta(S,Z_{1:T})].
 \label{eq:em}
\end{align}
For a finite HMM, the \emph{forward--backward algorithm} combines forward
probability calculations from the past with backward calculations from
future observations. It supplies expected transition and
emission counts; these replace the observed counts. For a linear Gaussian model,
the E-step uses smoothing means \emph{and second moments}, including
cross moments at adjacent times.

Exact EM makes the observed log likelihood nondecreasing. Under regularity
conditions its limit points are stationary: they satisfy first-order
optimality conditions, with zero gradient at an interior point.
Finite HMMs and linear Gaussian models admit efficient E- and M-steps.
\Cref{ex:l5-em} asks for the monotonicity calculation.

Another approach treats the hidden states as optimization variables:
\begin{equation}
 \min_{\theta,s_{0:T}}-\log Q_\theta(s_{0:T},Z_{1:T}).
 \label{eq:joint-map}
\end{equation}
This maximizes a joint mass or density, rather than summing or integrating
over states. Multiplying by a prior density on \(\theta\) gives a joint
\emph{maximum a posteriori} (MAP) objective: maximize the posterior
density of states and parameters given the observations.
Trajectory optimization is central in \emph{simultaneous localization
and mapping} (SLAM), where a robot estimates its path and a map from
measurements. Its objective is often sparse: each measurement involves
only a small subset of the unknown poses and map features.
It can remain nonconvex. For continuous states its density objective also
depends on the chosen coordinates and reference measure.

For deterministic linear state dynamics and unit Gaussian observation
noise, optimizing the initial state \(s=S_1\) gives
\[
 \min_{A,C,s}\sum_{t=1}^T\|Z_t-CA^{t-1}s\|_2^2.
\]
Products and powers of the unknown matrices make this a nonconvex
parameter-fitting problem.

For the observation-driven model in \cref{eq:observation-driven}, specified
parameters and \(R_1\) determine every \(R_t\) by a forward computation
on the observed prefix. Likelihood evaluation also needs the conditional
mass or density of \(j_\theta(R_t,W_t)\). For example,
\(j_\theta(r,w)=Cr+w\) with Gaussian noise of covariance \(\Omega\succ0\)
gives the evaluable density \(\mathcal N(Cr,\Omega)\).
A general observation-sampling map need not provide an efficient way to
evaluate its density. When the state updates and log densities are
differentiable, backpropagation differentiates their forward computation;
\cref{sec:rnn} returns to this calculation.

\section{Fit an observable predictor, then impose state-space structure}
\label{sec:observable-first}

The proposed two-stage method first fits a flexible predictor using
observed inputs and targets, then fits a compact state-space representation
to that predictor. The first stage avoids hidden-state optimization.
The second imposes relationships between predictions at different lags.

A finite-window model predicts from \(Z_{t-p:t-1}\). If older observations
have little additional predictive effect, choosing a sufficiently large
\(p\) controls approximation error. This is a statement about conditional
prediction.

Why fit a large, noisy model first? Many fitted coefficients can be noisy
estimates of quantities determined by a few shared parameters. Enforcing
those relationships can reduce estimation error. It also introduces bias
when the proposed structure is wrong. \Cref{ex:l5-sharing} makes the variance
reduction exact for coefficients constrained to a known low-dimensional
family. The subspace algorithm below also estimates the family from data.

Long memory and filtering stability can coexist. The physical state can
retain information while new observations correct errors in its estimate.
For the linear models below, different matrices govern the physical state
and errors in its observation-based estimate. The state may retain or
amplify past perturbations even while errors in its estimate decay.
\Cref{sec:memory-example} gives a process with permanent memory and an
exact compact predictor.

\section{Linear dynamical systems}
\label{sec:lds-model}

A \emph{linear dynamical system} (LDS) has state \(S_t\in\R^d\), observations \(Z_t\in\R^m\),
and equations
\begin{equation}
 S_{t+1}=AS_t+V_{t+1},\qquad
 Z_{t+1}=CS_{t+1}+W_{t+1}.
 \label{eq:linear-ssm}
\end{equation}
Here \(A\in\R^{d\times d}\), \(C\in\R^{m\times d}\).
The noise laws are specified separately; we state the Gaussian assumption
where it is used.
The observations are now continuous. KL still means relative entropy between
probability laws; when the laws have densities, sums become integrals of log
density ratios. It is not defined by treating differential entropy as ordinary
discrete entropy. \Cref{sec:continuous-kl} gives the precise definition and the
Gaussian formula used below.

An invertible coordinate change \(S_t'=T_0^{-1}S_t\) replaces the matrices by
\(A'=T_0^{-1}AT_0\), \(C'=CT_0\), and transforms the noise and initial
laws accordingly, without changing the observation law.
The relation between \(A\) and \(A'\) is called \emph{similarity}.

The \emph{spectral radius} \(\rho(A)\) is the largest absolute value of
an eigenvalue of \(A\). We call the linear recurrence \emph{stable} when
\(\rho(A)<1\), equivalently \(A^t\to0\): initial-state perturbations decay.
For a symmetric matrix \(\Sigma\), \(\Sigma\succ0\) means
\(x^\top\Sigma x>0\) for every nonzero \(x\); this is \emph{positive
definiteness}. Replacing \(>\) by \(\ge\) defines \(\Sigma\succeq0\).
We write \(\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\) for its smallest eigenvalue.

\begin{example}[Position and unobserved velocity]
\label{eg:position-velocity}
With sampling interval \(\Delta>0\), consider
\[
 S_t=\begin{pmatrix}P_t\\U_t\end{pmatrix},\quad
 A=\begin{pmatrix}1&\Delta\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad C=(1,0),
 \quad V_{t+1}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\\xi_{t+1}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The model takes \(P_{t+1}=P_t+\Delta U_t\), then perturbs velocity.
Only position is measured: \(Z_t=P_t+W_t\).
Differencing measurements estimates velocity, but also differences
measurement noise. For independent measurement errors of variance
\(\sigma_W^2\), the noise in
\((Z_{t+1}-Z_t)/\Delta\) has variance \(2\sigma_W^2/\Delta^2\).
A state estimator can combine many measurements instead.

Both eigenvalues of \(A\) are one. State perturbations need not decay,
and velocity errors accumulate in position.
The process-noise covariance is \emph{singular}: a nonzero direction
has zero noise variance. Here
\(\Sigma_V=\diag(0,\sigma_\xi^2)\).
This is appropriate for the discrete model just specified.\endnote{
\textbf{When does acceleration also perturb position?}
If a random acceleration acts throughout a sampling interval, integrating
it generally gives correlated position and velocity perturbations.
The zero position-noise convention corresponds to motion at the old
velocity followed by a random velocity change at the interval boundary.}
\end{example}

Adding a small covariance \(\varepsilon I\) makes a singular Gaussian
transition density nonsingular and can regularize numerical calculations.
This models additional state noise. Full-rank observation noise can give
a nonsingular observation law even with singular state-transition covariance.
If fitted variances are allowed
to collapse around interpolated data, a Gaussian likelihood can become
unbounded; an appropriate variance constraint or prior addresses that issue.

We will first justify the algorithm under positive-definite process and
measurement covariances and stable \(A\), which permit a short proof.
The bibliographical notes describe more general identification results.
\emph{Numerical conditioning} concerns the sensitivity of a
computed answer to perturbations in its inputs. Near-singular covariances
require checking this sensitivity in both prediction and parameter recovery.

\subsection{Finite HMMs also have linear vector equations}
\label{sec:vector-bridge}

For the finite HMM, the one-hot vectors \(e_{S_t}\in\R^d\) and
\(Z_t=e_{X_t}\in\R^N\) satisfy
\[
 e_{S_{t+1}}=Ae_{S_t}+V_{t+1},\qquad
 Z_{t+1}=Ce_{S_{t+1}}+W_{t+1}.
\]
These have the form of \cref{eq:linear-ssm}, with residuals satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \E[V_{t+1}\mid\mathcal G_t]=0,\qquad
 \E[W_{t+1}\mid\mathcal G_t,S_{t+1}]=0,\qquad
 \mathcal G_t=\sigma(S_{0:t},Z_{1:t}).
 \label{eq:hmm-noise}
\end{equation}
Here \(\sigma(S_{0:t},Z_{1:t})\) denotes the information generated by
the listed variables; conditioning on it means conditioning on their history.
For example, column \(j\) of \(A\) is the conditional mean of
\(e_{S_{t+1}}\) given \(S_t=j\); subtraction defines \(V_{t+1}\).
These additive residuals generally have state-dependent, non-Gaussian
laws. One-hot encoding gives linear conditional means.

More generally, for a finite-valued (\emph{categorical}) observation,
\begin{proposition}[A categorical conditional mean]
\label{prop:one-hot}
With \(\mathcal F_t=\sigma(X_{1:t})\) and
\(P_t=\E[e_{X_{t+1}}\mid\mathcal F_t]\),
\[
 (P_t)_a=Q(X_{t+1}=a\mid X_{1:t}),\qquad
 Z_{t+1}=P_t+E_{t+1},\qquad
 \E[E_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]=0.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Coordinate \(a\) of \(e_{X_{t+1}}\) is \(\I\{X_{t+1}=a\}\).
Take its conditional expectation, then subtract.
\end{proof}
A sequence with conditional mean zero given its past is called a
\emph{martingale-difference sequence}; the residuals above have this property. With
\(P_t=\phi_t(X_{1:t})\), this is a vector autoregression.
Squared loss against \(e_{X_{t+1}}\) is the Brier loss of Lecture~3.

\section{The regression and subspace algorithm}
\label{sec:subspace-algorithm}

We now give the complete procedure before explaining why it can work.
For centered (zero-mean) real observations, choose a history length \(p\), a block
count \(q\) with \(2q\le p\), and a retained state dimension \(1\le r\le qm\).
An intercept (constant prediction offset) or a mean model is needed when centering is inappropriate.
Vector norms below are Euclidean; matrix norms are induced operator norms
unless marked \({\rm F}\) for Frobenius norm.
Explicitly, \(\|A\|=\sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|Ax\|_2\) and
\(\|A\|_{\rm F}^2=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2\).

\subsection{Fit lag coefficients}

Use the observed lag vector
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_t=\begin{pmatrix}Z_t\\Z_{t-1}\\\vdots\\Z_{t-p+1}\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad \widehat B=(\widehat B_1,\ldots,\widehat B_p)
 \in\arg\min_B\sum_{t=p}^{T-1}\|Z_{t+1}-B\Psi_t\|_2^2 .
 \label{eq:observable-regression}
\end{equation}
Each \(B_j\in\R^{m\times m}\) maps one observed lag to the next
prediction; \(B\in\R^{m\times mp}\). Thus
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Z_{t+1}=\sum_{j=1}^p\widehat B_jZ_{t+1-j}.
 \label{eq:linear-ar}
\end{equation}
This is order-\(p\) \emph{linear autoregression}, abbreviated AR(\(p\)).
The \emph{design matrix} has rows \(\Psi_t^\top\), one for each fitted target.
\begin{proposition}[Observable fitting is convex]
\label{prop:observable-regression}
For fixed observed data, the objective in
\cref{eq:observable-regression} is convex in \(B\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each prediction is linear in \(B\); squared Euclidean distance is convex.
\end{proof}

This is conditional Gaussian maximum likelihood, conditional on the
first \(p\) observations, for iid \emph{isotropic} Gaussian residuals:
their covariance is a fixed multiple of the identity. With a specified covariance \(\Omega\succ0\), the loss is
the corresponding quadratic form
\(u^\top\Omega^{-1}u\). For unrestricted multioutput regression its
minimizer is the same ordinary least-squares coefficient matrix when the
design has full rank. Other conditional noise densities give other
negative-log-likelihood losses.

\subsection{Arrange, factor, and recover}
\label{sec:hankel}

Form two \(qm\times qm\) \emph{block Hankel matrices}:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\mathcal H}_0=[\widehat B_{i+j-1}]_{i,j=1}^q,\qquad
 \widehat{\mathcal H}_1=[\widehat B_{i+j}]_{i,j=1}^q .
 \label{eq:estimated-hankel}
\end{equation}
Each block is \(m\times m\); block rows and columns index successive
lags. Equal anti-diagonals use the same fitted coefficient.
This indexing explains the requirement \(2q\le p\).

In a \emph{singular value decomposition} (SVD), a matrix is written as
\(UDV^\top\), with orthonormal columns in \(U,V\) and positive singular
values on the diagonal of \(D\). Retain the largest \(r\) singular values
of \(\widehat{\mathcal H}_0\), assumed positive, and set
\[
 \widehat{\mathcal H}_{0,r}=U_rD_rV_r^\top
   =\mathcal O\mathcal R,\qquad
 \mathcal O=U_rD_r^{1/2},\quad
 \mathcal R=D_r^{1/2}V_r^\top .
\]
The factors have sizes \(qm\times r\) and \(r\times qm\).
The retained matrix \(\widehat{\mathcal H}_{0,r}\) is the \emph{truncated
SVD}, a best rank-\(r\) approximation in operator and Frobenius norm.
For a matrix with compact SVD \(UDV^\top\), its \emph{Moore--Penrose
pseudoinverse} is \(VD^{-1}U^\top\), denoted by a dagger; only positive
singular values are included. Set
\begin{equation}
 \overline F=\mathcal O^\dagger\widehat{\mathcal H}_1\mathcal R^\dagger,
 \qquad \overline C=\mathcal O_{1:m,:},\qquad
 \overline L=\mathcal R_{:,1:m}.
 \label{eq:recover-matrices}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\overline F\) updates an \(r\)-dimensional predictive state,
\(\overline C\) reads it out, and \(\overline L\) inserts an observation.
A state-update and readout rule implementing a given predictor is called
a \emph{realization} of that predictor.
This is a regression-based subspace-identification construction.

\subsection{Estimate innovations and generate}
\label{sec:innovations-generation}

Run the recovered predictor on observed data:
\begin{equation}
 \overline M_1=0,\qquad
 \widehat Z_t=\overline C\overline M_t,\qquad
 \overline M_{t+1}=\overline F\overline M_t+\overline LZ_t.
 \label{eq:recovered-predictor}
\end{equation}
The true \emph{innovation} is the one-step prediction error
\(E_t=Z_t-\E[Z_t\mid Z_{<t}]\). The fitted residuals
\(\widehat E_t=Z_t-\widehat Z_t\) estimate these innovations.
Use a \emph{calibration block} \(\mathcal I\): observations reserved for
estimating the residual law, rather than fitting the lag coefficients.
For its size \(n_{\rm cal}\), a
zero-mean innovations fit uses
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\Omega=\frac1{n_{\rm cal}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal I}
 \widehat E_t\widehat E_t^\top .
 \label{eq:innovation-covariance}
\end{equation}
Check residual means and temporal correlations; a persistent mean needs
a mean model. Discarding an initial transient or fitting the initial
predictive state can be appropriate. A \emph{covariance floor} replaces
eigenvalues below a chosen positive threshold by that threshold. This makes
\(\widehat\Omega\) positive definite when needed for a density.
Using a separate block reduces reuse of fitting noise, although contiguous
blocks from one trajectory are not independent.

Set \(\overline A=\overline F+\overline L\overline C\) and generate
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde M_1=0,\qquad
 \widetilde Z_t=\overline C\widetilde M_t+\widetilde E_t,\qquad
 \widetilde M_{t+1}=\overline A\widetilde M_t+\overline L\widetilde E_t,
 \quad \widetilde E_t\stackrel{\rm iid}{\sim}
 \mathcal N(0,\widehat\Omega).
 \label{eq:recovered-generator}
\end{equation}
This is an \emph{innovations realization}. Its state and observation
perturbations share the same \(\widetilde E_t\); they are correlated.
In the ideal case it gives the same observation law as the original LDS.
A process can have different state-space representations; independent
state and observation noises are not needed in this representation.
Substituting \(\widetilde E_t=\widetilde Z_t-\overline C\widetilde M_t\)
gives
\[
 \widetilde M_{t+1}
 =\overline F\widetilde M_t+\overline L\widetilde Z_t.
\]
This is the observation-driven update in \cref{fig:observed-feedback}:
the observation carries the noise into the next state.
Its state is an observation-based state estimate in suitable coordinates.
For the ideal linear Gaussian predictor, \(F=A-LC\).
Thus \(A\) governs generative state dynamics and \(F\) governs the
propagation of errors when filtering the same observed history. These
matrices can have different stability properties.

With exact infinite-history coefficients of a constant-gain Gaussian
predictor, the residuals are indeed iid Gaussian. For finite data,
truncated regression and estimated matrices, assess residual Gaussianity
and temporal correlations on held-out observations.
Moreover, \emph{population least squares} minimizes expected squared loss,
rather than a finite-data sum. Its finite-window coefficients generally
differ from the first \(p\) infinite-history coefficients. The omitted
tail can correlate with the included lags. Increasing \(p\) reduces
this bias under suitable filtering stability and design conditioning.

\subsection{What should be checked?}

Select \(p,q,r\) using held-out predictive performance and the intended
use. Inspect small retained singular values, residual covariance,
and both recovered matrices:
\[
 \rho(\overline F)\quad\hbox{for filtering},\qquad
 \rho(\overline A)\quad\hbox{for generation}.
\]
Truncated SVD alone does not guarantee
either stability condition. Predictive likelihood, generated statistics,
and stability diagnostics answer different questions.

The compact model ties all lag coefficients to
\(\overline C\overline F^{j-1}\overline L\). This is the parameter sharing
that the unconstrained first-stage regression omits. It can reduce
variance and extend a fitted finite history to a recurrent predictor.

\paragraph{Finite-window AR and variable-length predictors.}
The first-stage AR(\(p\)) fit always uses the same fixed window and learns a
separate matrix for each retained lag. The recovered state model instead uses
the same three matrices at every time:
\(
 M_{t+1}=\overline F M_t+\overline LZ_t
\)
and \(\widehat Z_{t+1}=\overline C M_{t+1}\).
It therefore defines one rule for prefixes of every length, while its
finite-dimensional state summarizes the entire observed prefix. Equivalently,
the few matrices generate the whole family of lag coefficients
\(\overline C\overline F^{j-1}\overline L\).

A causal transformer is closer to the state model than to fixed-window AR in
this limited sense. Shared layer parameters define a predictor for
variable-length prefixes, and attention can use every token inside the
available context rather than assigning one separately learned coefficient to
each fixed lag. The mechanisms remain different: a state model compresses the
prefix recursively into a fixed-dimensional state, whereas standard attention
retains token-level keys and values and revisits them. Its memory and
computation grow with the context, and using lengths beyond the architecture's
or training distribution's range is not automatic. The later discussion of
recurrent attention asks a separate computational question: when can an
attention calculation itself be reorganized as a recurrence or parallel scan?

\section{A controlled example of the memory advantage}
\label{sec:memory-example}

A state model can build a state that maintains information
from arbitrarily distant past,
whereas a fixed observation window discards past information.
Consider the scalar Gaussian process
\begin{equation}
 \begin{gathered}
 U\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2),\qquad S_0=U,\\
 S_{t+1}=S_t,\qquad Z_{t+1}=S_{t+1}+W_{t+1},\quad t\ge0,\\
 W_t\stackrel{\rm iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).
 \end{gathered}
 \label{eq:constant-state}
\end{equation}
with \(U\) independent of the noises and \(\tau,\sigma>0\).
The state in this process keeps the initial data indefinitely.
Suppose the model and its parameters are known and the goal is to predict
\(Z_{n+1}\) after observing \(Z_{1:n}\). The minimum-mean-squared-error
predictor is the posterior mean \(\E[S_n\mid Z_{1:n}]\).
The posterior mean and variance after \(n\)
observations are
\begin{equation}
 m_n=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i}{\sigma^2/\tau^2+n},\qquad
 v_n=\frac{\tau^2\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+n\tau^2}.
 \label{eq:constant-posterior}
\end{equation}
Letting \(H_n=\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i\), we obtain \(m_n\) from the updates
\(H_{t+1}=H_t+Z_{t+1}\) and \(C_{t+1}=C_t+1\), initialized with
\(H_0=0\) and \(C_0=\sigma^2/\tau^2\), and return \(m_n=H_n/C_n\).
Thus the optimal predictor stores two scalars rather than the full history.
\begin{proposition}[Full history versus a fixed window]
\label{prop:memory-advantage}
For \(n\ge p\), the minimum mean squared prediction error for \(Z_{n+1}\)
using all \(n\) observations is \(\sigma^2+v_n\).
Using only the most recent \(p\) observations gives minimum error
\(\sigma^2+v_p\), even among nonlinear predictors.
The expected KL from the full-history predictive law to the optimal
window predictive law is
\[
 \frac12\log\frac{\sigma^2+v_p}{\sigma^2+v_n}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Gaussian conditioning gives \cref{eq:constant-posterior}.
The conditional mean minimizes squared loss; the fresh measurement noise
adds \(\sigma^2\). Any \(p\) observations give the same posterior variance
\(v_p\), because their joint law is unchanged by reordering them.
Let \(a=\sigma^2+v_n\), \(b=\sigma^2+v_p\), and let \(m_p\) denote the
posterior mean based on the last \(p\) observations. Conditional-expectation
orthogonality gives
\[
 \E[(m_n-m_p)^2]
 =\E[(Z_{n+1}-m_p)^2]-\E[(Z_{n+1}-m_n)^2]
 =b-a.
\]
Applying the Gaussian KL formula in \cref{eq:gaussian-kl-scalar} conditionally
and averaging therefore leaves only \(\tfrac12\log(b/a)\).
\end{proof}

For \(\tau=\sigma=1\), \(p=10\), and \(n=1000\), the excess prediction
errors above irreducible noise are \(1/11\) and \(1/1001\).
This comparison uses known model parameters and isolates the effect of
history length. \Cref{ex:l5-memory} asks you to derive the posterior and
prediction formulas and to explain which additional data identify \(\tau^2\).

\subsection{A finite-data comparison of the actual algorithm}
\label{sec:synthetic-experiment}

A complementary experiment fits both models to the same single training
trajectory. The scalar innovations generator is
\[
 M_{t+1}=0.98M_t+0.18E_t,\qquad Z_t=M_t+E_t,\qquad
 E_t\stackrel{\rm iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,1),
\]
started at \(M_0=0\) and warmed up before the retained data.
Its predictor has \(F=0.8\) and coefficients \(K_j=0.18(0.8)^{j-1}\).
The lag-Hankel matrix has rank one.

We compare ordinary AR(32) with its rank-one subspace fit
(\(q=16,r=1\)); rank and history length are supplied, not selected
using test data. Both covariance estimates use a separate 2,000-step
calibration continuation. A further 10,000-step continuation evaluates
conditional-mean error and Gaussian conditional KL against the exact
innovations predictor. Repeating over 50 fixed random seeds measures
variation between training trajectories. Results, including generation
instability, appear in \cref{tab:l5-synthetic}.
The reproducible script is

For either predictor, let \(\widehat q(\cdot\mid Z_{<t})\) be its Gaussian
conditional density, with predicted mean \(\widehat\mu_t\) and calibrated
variance \(\widehat\Omega\). Its per-step test log-loss is
\[
 -\log\widehat q(Z_t\mid Z_{<t})
 =\frac12\log(2\pi\widehat\Omega)
  +\frac{(Z_t-\widehat\mu_t)^2}{2\widehat\Omega}.
\]
With a fixed positive variance, minimizing this loss over mean predictors
is equivalent to
minimizing squared error. The AR least-squares fit estimates the mean
coefficients; a residual-variance estimate completes the probability
model used for scoring and generation. Here both models estimate
\(\widehat\Omega\) on the calibration block. The ideal variance is
one for the exact state predictor and approximately
\(1+1.57\times10^{-7}\) for AR(32) with coefficients minimizing expected
squared error in the long-run limit; see \cref{app:synthetic-experiment}.
Thus one is the reference value for both methods in \cref{tab:l5-synthetic}.
For either fitted predictor, the data-generating law gives
\[
 \E[(Z_t-\widehat\mu_t)^2]
 =1+\E[(M_t-\widehat\mu_t)^2].
\]
Calibration includes mean-prediction error; a finite estimate can also
fall below one.

\paragraph{What does ``simpler'' mean?}
Suppose the constant-state structure in \cref{eq:constant-state} is supplied
to the learner. A direct state-model fit then estimates two variances, or an
equivalent tied prediction rule. Unrestricted AR(10) estimates ten regression
coefficients separately and, for a probabilistic model, a residual variance.
For a fixed number of independent training trajectories, our prior expectation
is therefore that the correctly specified state fit has the statistical
advantage: its parameter sharing reduces estimation variance. This expectation
does not follow from the number of time points alone. The distribution of
\(U\) is identified by variation between trajectories, so one long trajectory
is not equivalent to many independent draws of \(U\).

AR is simpler in a different sense. Least squares is a direct convex
calculation, whereas fitting a latent model can require nonconvex optimization
or a numerically delicate realization step. AR also imposes less of the
particular constant-state structure, which can help when that structure is
wrong. The rank-one model in \cref{fig:l5-synthetic} has fewer final
parameters than AR(32), yet the generic subspace procedure first estimates AR
coefficients and then performs an SVD-based realization; at \(T=256\), that
procedure is less reliable. Representation size, optimization difficulty,
conditioning, structural commitment, and prediction quality are distinct
notions of simplicity. They need not rank two methods in the same order.

Minimum description length (MDL) does not reduce this comparison to counting
parameters. It requires a specified class of candidate codes, including how
models or parameters and the data are encoded, and it selects the shortest
resulting description \citep{barron1998}. Familiar parameter-count penalties
are special asymptotic approximations for regular models. Latent-variable
models are often singular, where such approximations can fail
\citep{watanabe2013}. A fully specified MDL procedure may prefer either model;
its answer depends on the candidates, code, data, and prediction target. The
examples here contradict the slogan that small samples automatically favor
the model with fewer parameters. They do not contradict MDL as a precisely
specified model-selection method.

\section{Recurrent networks, backpropagation, and attention}
\label{sec:rnn}

A conventional recurrent predictor computes
\[
 R_{t+1}=h_\theta(R_t,u_t),\qquad
 q_\theta(\,\cdot\mid u_{1:t})=o_\theta(R_{t+1}).
\]
The inputs \(u_t\) may be external measurements or previous tokens.
With \emph{teacher forcing}, the supplied inputs are the observed past
tokens rather than sampled model outputs. The recurrent
states are deterministic functions of them, so the output likelihood
can be evaluated directly.

\emph{Backpropagation through time} differentiates this unrolled
computation. For a per-step update costing \(c\), its work is
\(O(Tc)\), up to constant factors for the derivatives; straightforward
storage is linear in \(T\) times the size of the intermediate values saved
per step. \emph{Checkpointing} saves only selected intermediate values
and recomputes the others during differentiation.
The state-update \emph{Jacobian} is its matrix of partial derivatives
with respect to the incoming state. Products of these matrices can make
gradients small or large. \emph{Long short-term memory} (LSTM) networks
use learned gates to control the retention and updating of state values;
gradient propagation depends on the resulting Jacobian products.

Linearity permits additional computational structure.
For affine updates with known per-position coefficients,
\[
 R_{t+1}=A_tR_t+b_t,\qquad
 (A_2,b_2)\circ(A_1,b_1)=(A_2A_1,A_2b_1+b_2),
\]
composition is associative. A \emph{parallel scan} computes all prefix
compositions by combining adjacent blocks in parallel. It can reduce
sequential depth, the number of operations that must wait for one another.
Dense matrix compositions may cost more than a sequential matrix--vector
recurrence; diagonal or other structured transitions make the scan useful.
Learning the matrices end to end can still be nonconvex.
The coefficients must be computable without first evaluating the unknown
preceding recurrent state. \Cref{ex:l5-bptt} compares these operations.

Observed inputs can also be added to an LDS:
\(S_{t+1}=AS_t+Bu_t+V_{t+1}\).
Lagged inputs become additional regression features. Inputs chosen
independently of unobserved system noises are simpler to analyze than
inputs correlated with those noises, for example through feedback.
Sufficient excitation still matters.

\subsection{An exact recurrent evaluation of linear attention}
\label{sec:attention-state}

Let key and query features \(k_t,q_t\in\R^r\), values \(v_t\in\R^s\),
and gates \(\gamma_t\in\R\) be computed from a layer's inputs.
The matrix state
\begin{equation}
 R_0=0,\qquad R_t=\gamma_tR_{t-1}+v_tk_t^\top,\qquad
 y_t=R_tq_t
 \label{eq:attention-recurrence}
\end{equation}
has \(sr\) coordinates.
\begin{proposition}[Recurrent and pairwise evaluations]
\label{prop:attention-recurrence}
The same output is
\[
 y_t=\sum_{u=1}^t v_u(k_u^\top q_t)
              \prod_{j=u+1}^t\gamma_j.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Unroll the affine recursion for \(R_t\) and multiply by \(q_t\).
The empty product equals one.
\end{proof}
For \(\gamma_t=1\), this is unnormalized \emph{causal linear attention}:
it uses only current and past values, with weights \(k_u^\top q_t\).
If these weights are nonnegative, a normalized version maintains
\(d_t=d_{t-1}+k_t\) and uses \(R_tq_t/(d_t^\top q_t)\), when the
denominator is positive. The state and recurrent work per token are
\(O(sr)\), in addition to feature computation. Directly evaluating
all pairwise interactions has quadratic dependence on sequence length.

This identity concerns a finite-feature attention map.
Unrestricted softmax attention need not admit such an exact fixed-size
summary.\endnote{\textbf{A rank obstruction.}
Choose scalar queries \(q_i=i-1\) and keys \(k_j=\log a_j\) for
distinct positive \(a_j\). Then
\([\exp(q_i k_j)]_{i,j=1}^n=[a_j^{i-1}]_{i,j=1}^n\) is an invertible
Vandermonde matrix. Row normalization preserves rank.
Putting these keys before these queries gives a past-to-future block
of causal attention with rank \(n\). A fixed \(r\)-feature bilinear
score matrix has rank at most \(r\), so it cannot represent all these blocks
for \(n>r\). This bound concerns finite-feature bilinear representations.}

\subsection{What this says about language models}

One can train recurrent-compatible layers from the start, or convert a
trained transformer using \emph{distillation}: train the new model to
match the original model's predictions or intermediate computations.
Mamba-2 develops structured state-space/attention computations with
efficient blocked implementations. MOHAWK matches attention-weight matrices,
intermediate representations, and predictions in stages.
Mamba-in-Llama reuses attention projections and trains hybrid models;
retained attention layers still store past keys and values in growing caches.

Hazan and collaborators' spectral filtering takes another route:
fit combinations of fixed temporal filters---weighted sums of past
observations---whose span approximates a
specified family of dynamical predictors. It can avoid identifying
a physical transition matrix. Spectral state-space sequence layers extend
this idea, and observation spectral filtering addresses prediction from
outputs. These methods should be distinguished from recovering
\((F,C,L)\) by the Hankel procedure.

A small recurrent state offers economical sequential inference.
It must also support the information retrieval, optimization, numerical
stability, and accelerator utilization needed by the task.
Training-time auxiliary objectives offer another use of state structure:
they can require hidden representations to predict future representations
at several time scales and then discard the auxiliary hierarchy for
generation. This imposes partial state-like constraints while retaining an
ordinary autoregressive generator; the bibliographical notes describe a
recent example.
The cited papers
demonstrate particular constructions and experiments; broad architectural
dominance remains an empirical question. The possibility of stronger
state-space models is a research motivation.

\section{Analysis of the autoregression--subspace method\suppmark}
\label{sec:optional-theory}
The following results justify the construction under explicit assumptions.
They may be read after the algorithm and classroom discussion.
For a random vector \(Y\), its \emph{root mean square} (RMS) norm is
\((\E[\|Y\|_2^2])^{1/2}\), also called its \(L^2\) norm.
\subsection{Gaussian prediction and truncation}
\label{sec:noisy-prediction}

For a Gaussian LDS, the conditional distribution can be computed by a
recursive predictor. Its state is an estimate obtained from the
observations. This gives an observable autoregression after unrolling.
The tail of its history coefficients controls the truncation error.

The observations in this subsection are real vectors. Log loss uses their
probability densities, and the probability and KL chain rules apply to these
conditional densities.

\begin{restatable}[A constant-gain Gaussian predictor]{theorem}{kalmanpredictor}
\label{thm:kalman}
Initialize the system at time \(1\) for this theorem.
Consider \(S_{t+1}=AS_t+V_{t+1}\), \(Z_t=CS_t+W_t\) for
\(t\ge1\), with spectral radius
\(\rho(A)<1\), independent centered Gaussian process and observation
noises with covariances \(\Sigma_V\succ0\) and \(\Sigma_W\succ0\).
There are matrices \(\Pi\succ0\) and \(L\) such that, when
\(S_1\sim\mathcal N(0,\Pi)\) independently of the noises, the recursion
\begin{equation}
 M_1=0,\qquad M_{t+1}=FM_t+LZ_t,\qquad F=A-LC
 \label{eq:kalman-predictor}
\end{equation}
satisfies
\begin{equation}
 M_t=\E[S_t\mid Z_{<t}],\qquad
 Z_t\mid Z_{<t}\sim\mathcal N(CM_t,\Omega),\qquad
 \Omega=C\Pi C^\top+\Sigma_W.
 \label{eq:kalman-conditional}
\end{equation}
Moreover, \(\rho(F)<1\) and the observations have uniformly bounded
second moments.
\end{restatable}

The recursion is the \emph{Kalman predictor}. The covariance \(\Pi\)
is chosen to make the \emph{gain} \(L\), which converts observation error
into a state correction, constant over time; \cref{app:kalman} constructs it
and proves the theorem. For an arbitrary Gaussian initialization,
the same conditional calculation gives time-dependent gains.
Positive noise covariance means positive variance in every nonzero
direction. A stable \(A\) permits partially observed states and arbitrary
\(C\) in this result.

Define the \emph{innovations} \(E_t=Z_t-CM_t\).
Their conditional law is always \(\mathcal N(0,\Omega)\), so they are
independent across time and independent of the previous observations.
Consequently, the same observation law can be generated by
\begin{equation}
 Z_t=CM_t+E_t,\qquad
 M_{t+1}=(F+LC)M_t+LE_t=AM_t+LE_t.
 \label{eq:innovations-generation}
\end{equation}
The conditional predictor and its innovations law together specify
generation.

Unrolling \cref{eq:kalman-predictor} gives
\begin{equation}
 CM_t=\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}K_jZ_{t-j},\qquad K_j=CF^{j-1}L.
 \label{eq:history-coefficients}
\end{equation}
Because \(F\) is stable, these coefficients decay geometrically.
The following proposition translates their tail into a sequence-law
guarantee.

\begin{proposition}[Truncation controls prediction and sequence KL]
\label{prop:truncation}
In the setting above, suppose
\(\lVert K_j\rVert\le G\gamma^{j-1}\) for \(j\ge1\), with
\(0<\gamma<1\), and
\(\sup_t(\E[\lVert Z_t\rVert_2^2])^{1/2}\le B_Z\).
Define
\[
 \widehat Z_t^{(p)}=\sum_{j=1}^{\min(p,t-1)}K_jZ_{t-j}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 \bigl(\E[\lVert CM_t-\widehat Z_t^{(p)}\rVert_2^2]\bigr)^{1/2}
 \le a_p:=\frac{GB_Z\gamma^p}{1-\gamma}.
 \label{eq:truncation-mean}
\end{equation}
Let \(P_{1:T}\) be the true observation law and let \(Q^{(p)}_{1:T}\)
use conditional law \(\mathcal N(\widehat Z_t^{(p)},\Omega)\).
Then
\begin{equation}
 \KL(P_{1:T}, Q^{(p)}_{1:T})
 \le\frac{T a_p^2}{2\lambda_{\min}(\Omega)}.
 \label{eq:truncation-kl}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The triangle inequality in \(L^2\) bounds the omitted tail by
\(B_Z\sum_{j>p}G\gamma^{j-1}=a_p\).
For Gaussians with the same covariance,
\[
 \KL(\mathcal N(u,\Omega),\mathcal N(v,\Omega))
 =\tfrac12(u-v)^\top\Omega^{-1}(u-v).
\]
Apply this at each observed prefix and use the KL chain rule.
\end{proof}

This explains why an observable autoregression can approximate a noisy
state-space model well: the lost remote history has an explicit cost.
These bounds use the true coefficients.
Regression contributes coefficient-estimation error in addition to
this truncation error.
Squared loss is convenient for the convex fit and the Gaussian
calculation; the state-elimination identity itself is independent of
the fitting loss.

\subsection{Exact recovery from ideal coefficients}
\label{sec:exact-realization}

The coefficients in \cref{eq:history-coefficients} come from a small
recurrent computation. Their matrix structure reveals that computation.
Suppose \(K_j=C_\star F_\star^{j-1}L_\star\) has minimal state
dimension \(d_\star\): no smaller state can realize the same sequence
of coefficients. Choose a block count \(q\) for which
\[
 \mathcal O_\star=
 \begin{pmatrix}C_\star\\C_\star F_\star\\\vdots\\C_\star F_\star^{q-1}\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \mathcal R_\star=
 (L_\star,F_\star L_\star,\ldots,F_\star^{q-1}L_\star)
\]
both have rank \(d_\star\). The first maps a state to successive
readouts; the second collects how successive observations enter the state.
Form the \(qm\times qm\) block matrices
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal H_0=[K_{i+j-1}]_{i,j=1}^q
   =\mathcal O_\star\mathcal R_\star,\qquad
 \mathcal H_1=[K_{i+j}]_{i,j=1}^q
   =\mathcal O_\star F_\star\mathcal R_\star.
 \label{eq:hankel-factorization}
\end{equation}
Each block is \(m\times m\); block rows and columns index successive
lags. A matrix constant along block anti-diagonals is a
\emph{block Hankel matrix}. Here \(\rank(\mathcal H_0)=d_\star\).

Take its rank-\(d_\star\) singular value decomposition and factor it:
\[
 \mathcal H_0=UDV^\top=\mathcal O\mathcal R,\qquad
 \mathcal O=UD^{1/2},\quad\mathcal R=D^{1/2}V^\top.
\]
Apply \cref{eq:recover-matrices} with the exact matrix
\(\mathcal H_1\) and these factors. Then the matrices satisfy the following
recovery property.

\begin{restatable}[Recovery of the prediction map]{proposition}{hankelrecovery}
\label{prop:hankel-recovery}
The recovered matrices satisfy
\[
 \overline C\,\overline F^{j-1}\overline L=K_j
 \quad\text{for every }j\ge1.
\]
Thus \cref{eq:recovered-predictor} computes the same predictor
on every observed history.
\end{restatable}

The proof in \cref{app:hankel} shows that the recovered states differ
from minimal predictive coordinates by an invertible change of basis.
Their role is to implement prediction; recovering the original physical
coordinates is optional.

\subsection{What finite-data guarantees must control}
\label{sec:finite-guarantees}

A finite-data analysis must control four errors:
finite-history approximation, estimation of the regression coefficients,
matrix realization, and estimation of the innovations law.

The regression error has a useful exact decomposition. Write
\[
 Z_{t+1}=B^{[p]}\Psi_t+r_t+E_{t+1},\qquad
 B^{[p]}=(K_1,\ldots,K_p),
\]
where \(r_t\) is the omitted predictor tail and \(E_{t+1}\) is the
innovation. If \(\sum_t\Psi_t\Psi_t^\top\) is invertible, ordinary least
squares gives
\begin{equation}
 \widehat B-B^{[p]}
 =\left(\sum_tE_{t+1}\Psi_t^\top+
         \sum_tr_t\Psi_t^\top\right)
       \left(\sum_t\Psi_t\Psi_t^\top\right)^{-1}.
 \label{eq:regression-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first numerator sums conditionally mean-zero terms; it is a
martingale sum. The second is truncation error correlated with the
regressors. The matrix \(\sum_t\Psi_t\Psi_t^\top\) is the design's
\emph{Gram matrix}. Its inverse expresses
\emph{excitation}: insufficient variation in some direction makes that
direction hard to estimate. Dependence between overlapping windows must
be handled in the probability bounds.

If every coefficient used in a \(q\)-block Hankel matrix has operator
error at most \(\delta\), its matrix error is at most \(q\delta\).
Indeed, each output block has norm at most
\(\delta\sum_j\|x_j\|\), and Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the full norm.
If the true Hankel matrix has rank \(r\), the best rank-\(r\)
approximation to its estimate obeys
\begin{equation}
 \|\widehat{\mathcal H}_{0,r}-\mathcal H_0\|
 \le2\|\widehat{\mathcal H}_0-\mathcal H_0\|.
 \label{eq:rank-truncation}
\end{equation}
This follows because the discarded singular value is no larger than
the perturbation norm. Converting this to matrices requires controlling
\(\sigma_r(\mathcal H_0)\) and the pseudoinverses.
Prediction-risk bounds also require the conversions in the next subsection.

There are finite-sample theorems for related stochastic subspace
algorithms, including single-trajectory recovery up to similarity at
order \(T^{-1/2}\) with logarithmic and system-dependent factors.
Some allow \(\rho(A)\le1\). Their assumptions include
\emph{observability}: different noiseless initial states can be distinguished
by their observations, equivalently \((C^\top,(CA)^\top,\ldots,(CA^{d-1})^\top)^\top\)
has rank \(d\). They also require \emph{controllability by the process noise}:
\((\Sigma_V^{1/2},A\Sigma_V^{1/2},\ldots,A^{d-1}\Sigma_V^{1/2})\) has rank \(d\),
so process noise can affect every state direction over time.
Other assumptions include positive measurement covariance, known state
dimension, and a \emph{steady-state filtering initialization}, whose
prediction-error covariance is fixed by the covariance recursion.
Their regression predicts a block of future observations from a block
of past observations, rather than the single next observation used here.
The bibliographical notes give the exact source and theorem locations.
A corresponding rate for the algorithm here requires analysis of
\cref{eq:regression-decomposition} and its realization step.

\subsection{From matrix errors to prediction and sequence laws}
\label{sec:metric-conversions}

The following calculation makes a useful part of the conversion explicit.
Compare a true and fitted predictor on the \emph{same observed history},
in aligned state coordinates:
\[
 M_{t+1}=FM_t+LZ_t,\qquad
 \overline M_{t+1}=\overline F\overline M_t+\overline LZ_t,
 \qquad M_1=\overline M_1=0.
\]
Suppose \(\|\overline F\|\le\rho<1\), the true states and observations
have RMS norms bounded by \(B_M,B_Z\), and
\(\|\overline F-F\|\le\delta_F\),
\(\|\overline L-L\|\le\delta_L\),
\(\|\overline C-C\|\le\delta_C\).
Then the RMS state discrepancy
\(e_t=(\E[\|\overline M_t-M_t\|^2])^{1/2}\) obeys
\[
 e_{t+1}\le\rho e_t+\delta_FB_M+\delta_LB_Z.
\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}
 \sup_t\bigl(\E[\|\overline C\overline M_t-CM_t\|^2]\bigr)^{1/2}
 \le \varepsilon_\mu:=
 \frac{\|\overline C\|(\delta_FB_M+\delta_LB_Z)}{1-\rho}
       +\delta_CB_M .
 \label{eq:parameter-to-prediction}
\end{equation}
Here \emph{contraction} means shrinking distances by a factor strictly
below one. A weighted norm \(\|x\|_H=(x^\top Hx)^{1/2}\), \(H\succ0\),
may supply contraction when the ordinary Euclidean norm does not.
Alignment and norm conditioning
then affect the constants.

For Gaussian conditional laws, mean accuracy and covariance accuracy
together give sequence KL.
\begin{proposition}[Conditional errors control Gaussian sequence KL]
\label{prop:gaussian-sequence-kl}
Let \(P\) and \(Q\) have conditional laws
\(\mathcal N(\mu_t,\Omega)\) and
\(\mathcal N(\widehat\mu_t,\widehat\Omega)\), evaluated at the same
prefix, with positive-definite constant covariances. Then
\[
 \KL(P_{1:T},Q_{1:T})
 =\frac12\sum_{t=1}^T\E_P[
   (\mu_t-\widehat\mu_t)^\top\widehat\Omega^{-1}
   (\mu_t-\widehat\mu_t)]
 +\frac T2\left[
 \operatorname{tr}(\widehat\Omega^{-1}\Omega)-m+
 \log\frac{\det\widehat\Omega}{\det\Omega}\right].
\]
If \(\E_P[\|\mu_t-\widehat\mu_t\|^2]\le\varepsilon_\mu^2\)
and all eigenvalues of
\(\widehat\Omega^{-1/2}\Omega\widehat\Omega^{-1/2}\)
lie in \([1-\eta,1+\eta]\), \(0\le\eta<1\), then
\begin{equation}
 \frac1T\KL(P_{1:T},Q_{1:T})
 \le \frac{\varepsilon_\mu^2}{2\lambda_{\min}(\widehat\Omega)}
       +\frac{m\eta^2}{4(1-\eta)^2}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-kl-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply the sequence KL chain rule and the Gaussian KL formula.
For \(\lambda\in[1-\eta,1+\eta]\), Taylor's theorem gives
\(\lambda-1-\log\lambda\le(\lambda-1)^2/[2(1-\eta)^2]\).
Sum this bound over the covariance eigenvalues.
\end{proof}

This explains which small-noise issue matters for KL:
mean errors are measured in units of conditional observation variance.
A nearly zero \(\lambda_{\min}(\widehat\Omega)\) can make a small
Euclidean error costly. The conditional observation covariance can remain
positive definite when the physical process-noise covariance is singular.

For any sequence cost with range length \(C_{\max}\), Pinsker gives
\[
 |\E_P[c]-\E_Q[c]|
 \le C_{\max}\sqrt{\KL(P_{1:T},Q_{1:T})/2}.
\]
The sequence KL bound grows with \(T\). To control long-run generated statistics, use stability
of the \emph{generative} dynamics and an appropriate \emph{coupling}:
a joint construction of the two processes with their prescribed marginal laws.
\Cref{app:state-stability} gives that comparison.
The required condition concerns \(\overline A=\overline F+\overline L\overline C\),
the matrix governing generation.

\subsection{Stable autoregression can also control long-run error}
\label{sec:stable-ar}

Stability can be imposed directly on the observable regression.
Here is one sufficient condition that preserves convex fitting.

\begin{proposition}[A stable autoregressive fit]
\label{prop:stable-ar}
Let \(Z_{t+1}=P_t+E_{t+1}\), where \(P_t\) is its conditional mean
and the \(E_t\) are iid additive innovations independent of the past.
Fix fitted coefficient matrices satisfying
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^p\lVert\widehat B_j\rVert\le\beta<1,\qquad
 \sup_{t\ge p}
 \left(\E\left[
 \left\lVert P_t-\sum_{j=1}^p\widehat B_jZ_{t+1-j}\right\rVert_2^2
 \right]\right)^{1/2}\le\varepsilon.
\]
Start a fitted generator from the same first \(p\) observations and
use the same innovations in a coupling:
\[
 \widetilde Z_{t+1}
 =\sum_{j=1}^p\widehat B_j\widetilde Z_{t+1-j}+E_{t+1}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 \sup_t\bigl(\E[\lVert Z_t-\widetilde Z_t\rVert_2^2]\bigr)^{1/2}
 \le\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\beta}.
 \label{eq:stable-ar}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(d_t\) denote the RMS discrepancy. Inserting the fitted predictor
evaluated on the true history and using the \(L^2\) triangle inequality
gives \(d_{t+1}\le\varepsilon+\sum_j\lVert\widehat B_j\rVert d_{t+1-j}\).
Starting from \(d_1=\cdots=d_p=0\), induction gives the result.
\end{proof}

The constraint on the sum of operator norms is convex.
A statistic \(c\) is \(L_c\)-\emph{Lipschitz} if
\(|c(x)-c(y)|\le L_c\|x-y\|\).
Such a statistic of one observation, or its time average,
then has expectation discrepancy at most
\(L_c\varepsilon/(1-\beta)\).
\Cref{app:state-stability} gives the corresponding state-space comparison
and a second-moment bound.
Thus both state-space modeling and autoregression can use stability.
The choice concerns how useful memory is represented, how much is
truncated, and what constraints the fitting method preserves.

\subsection{A noiseless comparison: Cayley--Hamilton}
\label{sec:cayley-hamilton}

In a noiseless LDS, we can eliminate the hidden state to obtain an exact
recurrence involving only observations. The key linear-algebra result
is the following.

\begin{restatable}[Cayley--Hamilton]{theorem}{cayleyhamilton}
\label{thm:cayley-hamilton}
Let \(A\in\R^{d\times d}\) have characteristic polynomial
\[
 \chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)
 =\lambda^d+a_{d-1}\lambda^{d-1}+\cdots+a_1\lambda+a_0.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 \chi_A(A)=A^d+a_{d-1}A^{d-1}+\cdots+a_1A+a_0I=0.
 \label{eq:cayley-hamilton}
\end{equation}
\end{restatable}

The proof by the adjugate identity is in \cref{app:cayley-hamilton}.

\begin{corollary}[Eliminating a noiseless hidden state]
\label{cor:exact-ar}
If \(S_{t+1}=AS_t\) and \(Z_t=CS_t\), then
\begin{equation}
 Z_{t+d}+a_{d-1}Z_{t+d-1}+\cdots+a_0Z_t=0.
 \label{eq:exact-ar}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The left side is \(C\chi_A(A)S_t=0\).
\end{proof}

The same scalar coefficients \(a_0,\ldots,a_{d-1}\) apply to every
observation coordinate, even when \(Z_t\) is vector-valued.
This deterministic linear model is more expressive than it may first appear.
Its trajectories are combinations of terms of the form
\(t^k\lambda^t\), arising from the eigenvalues and Jordan blocks of \(A\).
They therefore include exponential growth and decay, polynomial trends,
damped or persistent oscillations, periodic motion, and superpositions of
oscillations with incommensurate frequencies. Even dynamics that retain
information indefinitely have the recurrence in \cref{eq:exact-ar}.

Nonlinear state updates permit qualitatively different behavior, including
multiple attractors and chaos. A precise one-dimensional result illustrates
the distinction: if a continuous map from an interval to itself has a point
of least period three, then it has points of every positive period and an
uncountable scrambled set in the sense of Li and Yorke
\citep{li1975}. The conclusion requires continuity, an interval state space,
and least period three; ``period three implies chaos'' is not a statement
about an arbitrary nonlinear recurrence. The observation map can reveal or
hide features of the state dynamics.

With noise, the linear elimination produces additional, generally correlated
noise terms.
To fit a predictor by regression, we need its conditional mean
given the \emph{observations}, as in \cref{sec:noisy-prediction}.


\section{Take-home messages}
\label{sec:take-home}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=.2em]
\item A useful state-space model restricts what is remembered and how it
evolves. The observation process need not be Markov.
\item Language may be viewed as a partial observation of an evolving internal
process, which motivates latent state. This does not imply that explicitly
estimating a stochastic latent trajectory is the best predictive method.
\item Conditional generation first infers a distribution over the current
state. Filtering, smoothing, and most likely path decoding answer
different questions.
\item Hidden-state likelihood can be evaluated efficiently in important
families, while its optimization remains difficult. EM uses posterior
averages; joint state optimization uses a different objective.
\item Observable regression supplies a flexible predictor. Hankel
factorization imposes shared dynamics and can recover a compact
innovations realization. Finite-data success depends on truncation,
excitation, rank conditioning, and residual modeling.
\item Fixed-window AR discards older observations. State models and
transformers instead share parameters across prefix lengths, but state models
compress the prefix into fixed-dimensional recurrent memory while standard
attention retains token-level memory that grows with the context.
\item Memory in the physical state and forgetting of filtering errors
are compatible. Prediction stability and generation stability must
be checked separately.
\item Deterministic recurrent states permit backpropagation through
observed prefixes. Structured affine updates can also support parallel
training and recurrent evaluation of some attention maps.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Filtering, smoothing, and likelihood fitting.}
\citet{doucet2009} give an accessible particle-filtering and smoothing
tutorial. \citet{douc2014} establishes time-uniform
control of asymptotic particle-filter variance and error under mixing
and observation assumptions, including noncompact state spaces.

Dynamax implements EM and gradient-based marginal-likelihood fitting
\citep{dynamax}. Thus differentiating a forward recursion is an established
computational option. \citet{wu1983} supplies convergence conditions for EM
and explains why monotonicity alone does not establish convergence to
a local optimum. \citet{dellaert2017} develops sparse trajectory MAP
estimation for robotics. It concerns optimization over states and, in
SLAM, map variables, rather than replacing marginal likelihood by an
equivalent objective.

\paragraph{Gaussian prediction and realization.}
\citet{mikusheva2009} derives Gaussian state prediction.
The sufficient-condition proof in \cref{app:kalman} is included in full.
\citet{hokalman1966} gives the classical realization construction from
input-output coefficient matrices; \cref{sec:exact-realization} applies
that algebra to the predictor's coefficients.

For finite stochastic data, \citet{tsiamis2019}, Theorem~1 and Theorem~4
with Remark~4, analyzes future-on-past block regression and an SVD-based
realization. Assumptions~1--3 specify known state dimension, observability,
controllability by process noise, positive measurement covariance,
steady-state filtering initialization, and sufficient block lengths.
Together with sample-size and conditioning requirements, the bounds give
matrix error of order \(T^{-1/2}\) up to logarithmic factors and similarity.
The allowed \(\rho(A)\le1\) includes the boundary case \(\rho(A)=1\).
This is a closely related algorithm, not a finite-sample theorem for
every one-step AR/SVD variant.

\citet{tsiamis2020} instead bounds \emph{regret}, cumulative squared loss
minus that of the Kalman predictor, using online regression.
With high probability the bound grows as a power of \(\log T\),
including systems with \(\rho(A)\le1\) under the paper's
conditions. This directly addresses prediction performance; it need
not identify a compact generative realization. The Gaussian KL and
coupling calculations here explain additional steps needed when the
evaluation criterion is a sequence law or a generated statistic.

\paragraph{Synthetic comparisons and spectral prediction.}
Synthetic dynamical systems are a standard controlled setting for
comparing sequence models. \citet{agarwal2024}, Section~4.1 and
Appendix~E.1, tests a system with diagonal eigenvalues \(\pm0.9999\)
and compares spectral units with learned linear recurrence.
These eigenvalues are strictly inside the unit circle, although the
appendix calls the system marginally stable.
Our constant-state calculation isolates the effect of history length;
the AR/SVD experiment compares two fitted predictors.

\citet{hazan2017} fits a predictor in a fixed spectral feature space and
proves guarantees against a specified LDS class. The learner need not
recover the comparator's state coordinates. \citet{agarwal2024} develops
spectral sequence layers and an autoregressive extension.
\citet{dogariu2025} extends observable-history prediction using spectral
filtering and linear observers in a nonlinear-dynamics setting; the
bounds require their structural and conditioning assumptions.
These are additional ways to exploit dynamical structure.

\paragraph{Recurrent computation and language models.}
\citet{pascanu2013} analyzes vanishing and exploding gradients in
recurrent-network training. \citet{katharopoulos2020} derives recurrent
causal linear attention. \citet{dao2024}, Sections~2 and~5, develops
structured state-space duality and efficient matrix-based algorithms;
the finite-feature calculation in \cref{sec:attention-state} is an
elementary example.

\citet{bick2024} introduces MOHAWK's staged transformer-to-SSM distillation.
\citet{wang2024} converts pretrained attention layers and trains
hybrid recurrent/attention models. Their measured results concern the
models, tasks, and compute settings in those papers.

\citet{shi2026hilp} introduce Hierarchical Latent Prediction, which adds
auxiliary objectives requiring token-level and coarser hidden
representations to predict future hidden representations at several temporal
scales. These are partial state-like constraints on the shared transformer
representation. The auxiliary hierarchy is removed at inference, so
generation still uses the ordinary next-token head. The paper reports
improvements on its tested coding, multi-step reasoning, and speculative
decoding evaluations.

\paragraph{Nonlinear recurrence and chaos.}
\citet{li1975} prove the theorem summarized after
\cref{cor:exact-ar}: for a continuous self-map of an interval, a point of
least period three implies points of every positive period and an uncountable
scrambled set. This result motivates the standard phrase ``period three
implies chaos,'' with the stated setting and Li--Yorke notion of chaos.

\section*{Glossary}
\phantomsection
\label{sec:glossary}

\begingroup\small
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5em,labelindent=0pt,
  itemsep=0.15em,parsep=0pt]
\item[State-space model; latent state]
A model of observations through an evolving, possibly unobserved
state. State coordinates specify the information retained over time.
\item[Transition and observation kernels]
Conditional probability laws of the next state given the current state,
and of the current observation given the current state. The observation
kernel is also called the emission kernel; it averages over observation noise.
\item[One-hot encoding; martingale difference]
\(Z_t=e_{X_t}\) turns token probabilities into vector conditional means.
Subtracting a conditional mean produces a residual with conditional
expectation zero given the past.
\item[Linear dynamical system; linear autoregression]
An LDS has linear state and observation equations. A linear
autoregression predicts from a linear combination of observed lags.
\item[Unit-delay box \(\mathsf D\)]
A diagram element that stores its incoming signal for one time step.
Function boxes are static maps; feedback through a delay gives the diagram
memory.
\item[System identification]
Estimating dynamical and observation equations from observed data.
\item[Innovation; Kalman predictor]
An innovation is the observation minus its conditional-mean prediction.
For the Gaussian LDS considered here, the Kalman predictor computes
that mean recursively, and innovations are independent Gaussian vectors.
\item[Truncation error]
The predictive effect of history omitted by a finite-lag approximation.
It is controlled by the tail of the history coefficients.
\item[Hankel factorization]
Factoring a matrix of lag coefficients to recover a finite-dimensional
recurrent predictor. Its rank expresses the minimal predictive state
dimension under the stated rank assumptions.
\item[Filtering; prediction; smoothing]
Conditioning a current state on observations through the current time,
conditioning a future state on earlier observations, and conditioning
a state on observations before and after it.
\item[Observation-driven state]
A recurrent state updated deterministically using observations; given
parameters and its initial state, the observed prefix determines it.
\item[EM]
Alternating posterior inference and expected complete-data likelihood
maximization.
\item[Excitation; realization conditioning]
Variation in observed regressors and separation of retained singular
values from zero; both affect recovery from noisy data.
\item[Backpropagation through time]
Differentiation of the unrolled recurrent computation.
\end{description}
\endgroup

\section{Deferred proofs and stability comparisons}

\subsection{KL for continuous laws}
\label{sec:continuous-kl}

For probability laws \(P\) and \(Q\) on the same measurable space, suppose
that \(P\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(Q\), written
\(P\ll Q\). Then the density ratio \(dP/dQ\) exists and
\[
 \KL(P,Q)=\int \log\!\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)dP.
\]
If \(P\not\ll Q\), set \(\KL(P,Q)=+\infty\). When both laws have densities
\(p,q\) with respect to Lebesgue measure, this becomes
\[
 \KL(P,Q)=\int p(z)\log\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\,dz.
\]
This definition applies directly to discrete, continuous, and mixed laws; it
does not require defining differential entropy. It follows the treatment of
relative entropy on general measurable spaces in \citet[Chapter~14]{lattimore2020}.

For scalar Gaussian laws with \(a,b>0\), direct integration gives
\begin{equation}
 \KL\!\left(\mathcal N(\mu,a),\mathcal N(\nu,b)\right)
 =\frac12\left(
   \log\frac ba+\frac{a+(\mu-\nu)^2}{b}-1
 \right).
 \label{eq:gaussian-kl-scalar}
\end{equation}
For random conditional laws, expected conditional KL means that this
pointwise KL is averaged over the conditioning history.

\subsection{The constant-gain Gaussian predictor}
\label{app:kalman}

\kalmanpredictor*
\begin{proof}
We construct the covariance used in \cref{thm:kalman}.
If \(S_t\mid Z_{<t}\sim\mathcal N(M_t,\Pi_t)\), Gaussian conditioning
and the state equation give
\begin{align}
 \Omega_t&=C\Pi_tC^\top+\Sigma_W,\qquad
 L_t=A\Pi_tC^\top\Omega_t^{-1},\nonumber\\
 M_{t+1}&=AM_t+L_t(Z_t-CM_t),\label{eq:kalman-general}\\
 \Pi_{t+1}
 &=A\Pi_tA^\top+\Sigma_V
   -A\Pi_tC^\top\Omega_t^{-1}C\Pi_tA^\top.\nonumber
\end{align}
The covariance update is a deterministic map \(\mathcal T(\Pi_t)\).
To show that it has a fixed point under the stated conditions, use
the identity
\begin{equation}
 (A-GC)\Pi(A-GC)^\top+\Sigma_V+G\Sigma_WG^\top
 =\mathcal T(\Pi)+(G-L)(C\Pi C^\top+\Sigma_W)(G-L)^\top,
 \label{eq:riccati-minimum}
\end{equation}
where \(L=A\Pi C^\top(C\Pi C^\top+\Sigma_W)^{-1}\).
For any vector, its quadratic form on the left is minimized over
\(G\) by \(G=L\).
It follows that \(\mathcal T\) is monotone in the positive-semidefinite
order.

Since \(\rho(A)<1\), the finite matrix
\[
 B=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}A^j\Sigma_V(A^\top)^j
\]
satisfies \(B=ABA^\top+\Sigma_V\).
Starting from \(\Pi_0=0\), iterate \(\Pi_{n+1}=\mathcal T(\Pi_n)\).
Monotonicity gives an increasing sequence, and
\(\mathcal T(B)\preceq ABA^\top+\Sigma_V=B\) bounds it above.
It therefore converges to a fixed point \(\Pi\).
Continuity follows from \(\Sigma_W\succ0\), and
\(\Pi\succeq\Sigma_V\succ0\).

Set \(L=A\Pi C^\top(C\Pi C^\top+\Sigma_W)^{-1}\) and \(F=A-LC\).
The fixed-point identity becomes
\[
 \Pi=F\Pi F^\top+\Sigma_V+L\Sigma_WL^\top.
\]
Thus \(\Pi-F\Pi F^\top\succ0\); conjugation by \(\Pi^{-1/2}\)
shows that \(\rho(F)<1\).
Initialize \(S_1\sim\mathcal N(0,\Pi)\).
Induction in \cref{eq:kalman-general} now keeps the error covariance
equal to \(\Pi\), proving \cref{eq:kalman-predictor,eq:kalman-conditional}.

Finally,
\[
 S_t=A^{t-1}S_1+\sum_{j=2}^t A^{t-j}V_j.
\]
The geometric decay of powers of \(A\) bounds its second moment
uniformly in \(t\); \(Z_t=CS_t+W_t\) has the same property.
Geometric decay of powers of \(F\) also supplies the constants
\(G,\gamma\) in \cref{prop:truncation}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why the Hankel factors recover the predictor}
\label{app:hankel}

\hankelrecovery*
\begin{proof}
Under the ranks assumed in \cref{sec:exact-realization}, both
\(\mathcal H_0=\mathcal O_\star\mathcal R_\star\) and
\(\mathcal H_0=\mathcal O\mathcal R\) are full-rank factorizations
through a \(d_\star\)-dimensional space.
Their left factors have the same column space, so
\(\mathcal O=\mathcal O_\star T_0\) for an invertible \(T_0\).
Multiplying by a left inverse of \(\mathcal O_\star\) gives
\(\mathcal R=T_0^{-1}\mathcal R_\star\).
Consequently, \cref{eq:recover-matrices} yields
\[
 \overline F=T_0^{-1}F_\star T_0,\qquad
 \overline C=C_\star T_0,\qquad
 \overline L=T_0^{-1}L_\star.
\]
Substituting these into
\(\overline C\overline F^{j-1}\overline L\)
proves \cref{prop:hankel-recovery}.
Small singular values make inversion sensitive to coefficient errors;
the truncated SVD selects which directions to retain.
\end{proof}

\subsection{State-space error and long-run statistics}
\label{app:state-stability}

Consider two state processes on one probability space:
\[
 S_{t+1}=g(S_t)+V_{t+1},\qquad
 \widetilde S_{t+1}=\widehat g(\widetilde S_t)+\widetilde V_{t+1}.
\]
Assume that \(\widehat g\) is \(\rho\)-Lipschitz, \(0\le\rho<1\),
and that the local coupled RMS error is bounded:
\[
 \left(\E\left[
 \lVert g(S_t)+V_{t+1}
 -\widehat g(S_t)-\widetilde V_{t+1}\rVert_2^2
 \right]\right)^{1/2}\le\varepsilon.
\]
With \(e_t=(\E[\lVert S_t-\widetilde S_t\rVert_2^2])^{1/2}\),
inserting \(\widehat g(S_t)+\widetilde V_{t+1}\) between the next
states gives \(e_{t+1}\le\varepsilon+\rho e_t\).
Iterating gives the same geometric-sum bound used in Lecture~4:
\[
 e_t\le\rho^{t-1}e_1+
 \varepsilon\frac{1-\rho^{t-1}}{1-\rho}.
\]

For observations \(Z_t=r(S_t)+W_t\) and
\(\widetilde Z_t=\widehat r(\widetilde S_t)+\widetilde W_t\),
assume that \(\widehat r\) is \(L_r\)-Lipschitz and
\[
 \left(\E\left[
 \lVert r(S_t)+W_t-\widehat r(S_t)-\widetilde W_t\rVert_2^2
 \right]\right)^{1/2}\le b.
\]
The observation discrepancy satisfies
\[
 d_t:=(\E[\lVert Z_t-\widetilde Z_t\rVert_2^2])^{1/2}
 \le L_re_t+b.
\]
For an \(L_f\)-Lipschitz function \(f\),
\[
 |\E[f(Z_t)]-\E[f(\widetilde Z_t)]|\le L_fd_t.
\]
If both observation processes have RMS norm at most \(B_Z\), then
\begin{equation}
 \left\lVert\E[Z_tZ_s^\top]
 -\E[\widetilde Z_t\widetilde Z_s^\top]\right\rVert_{\rm F}
 \le B_Z(d_t+d_s).
 \label{eq:second-moment-control}
\end{equation}
To see this, expand the matrix difference using
\((Z_t-\widetilde Z_t)Z_s^\top+
\widetilde Z_t(Z_s-\widetilde Z_s)^\top\), and apply Cauchy--Schwarz.
Uniformly small coupled error therefore controls means, Lipschitz
costs, and fixed-lag second moments.

For linear Gaussian models in common state coordinates, take
\(V_t=\Sigma_V^{1/2}\Xi_t\) and
\(\widetilde V_t=\widehat\Sigma_V^{1/2}\Xi_t\) with shared standard
Gaussian \(\Xi_t\), and couple the observation noises similarly.
If the true states have RMS norm at most \(B_S\), sufficient local bounds are
\begin{align*}
 \varepsilon&\le\lVert A-\widehat A\rVert B_S+
 \lVert\Sigma_V^{1/2}-\widehat\Sigma_V^{1/2}\rVert_{\rm F},\\
 b&\le\lVert C-\widehat C\rVert B_S+
 \lVert\Sigma_W^{1/2}-\widehat\Sigma_W^{1/2}\rVert_{\rm F}.
\end{align*}
The contraction condition is \(\lVert\widehat A\rVert<1\).
A change of coordinates can express contraction in a weighted
Euclidean norm. These bounds control errors in observation statistics
uniformly over time.

\subsection{Cayley--Hamilton}
\label{app:cayley-hamilton}

\cayleyhamilton*
\begin{proof}
The \emph{adjugate} is the transpose of the matrix of cofactors.
Its defining determinant identity gives
\[
 (\lambda I-A)\operatorname{adj}(\lambda I-A)=\chi_A(\lambda)I.
\]
Write the adjugate, a matrix polynomial of degree at most \(d-1\), as
\(\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\lambda^jD_j\).
Comparing coefficients yields
\[
 D_{d-1}=I,\qquad
 D_{j-1}-AD_j=a_jI\ (1\le j\le d-1),\qquad
 -AD_0=a_0I.
\]
Multiply the middle identities on the left by \(A^j\), add them
to the last identity and to \(A^dD_{d-1}=A^d\).
All the \(D_j\) terms cancel, leaving \(\chi_A(A)=0\).
\end{proof}

\section{Reproducing the synthetic experiment}
\label{app:synthetic-experiment}

This appendix specifies the experiment in \cref{tab:l5-synthetic,fig:l5-synthetic}.
The saved code fits the AR and state-space models to the same observations
and evaluates them on the same continuation.

\subsection{Generator, seeds, and data blocks}

Use training sizes \(T\in\{256,1024,4096\}\) and seeds
\(s\in\{0,\ldots,49\}\). For each pair, initialize NumPy's
\texttt{default\_rng(s)} and draw independent standard normal
\(E_0,\ldots,E_{N-1}\), where \(N=T+14000\).
Using the code's zero-based indexing, generate
\[
 M_0=0,\qquad Z_t=M_t+E_t,\qquad
 M_{t+1}=0.98M_t+0.18E_t,\quad 0\le t<N.
\]
Equivalently, \(M_{t+1}=0.8M_t+0.18Z_t\), so \(M_t\) is the
exact conditional mean given \(Z_{0:t-1}\). The conditional variance
of \(Z_t\) is one. The data blocks are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Block & Indices (inclusive) & Length\\
\midrule
Warmup & \(0,\ldots,1999\) & 2000\\
Training & \(2000,\ldots,1999+T\) & \(T\)\\
Calibration & \(2000+T,\ldots,3999+T\) & 2000\\
Test & \(4000+T,\ldots,13999+T\) & 10000\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Only the training block is used to fit lag coefficients. The warmup
observations remain available as history when running the fitted
predictors. Their states are propagated from time zero through all
blocks without resetting at block boundaries. Calibration and test
are consecutive parts of the same trajectory. Reusing each seed across
training sizes makes those comparisons dependent; the 50 seeds supply
the repeated trials at each size.

\subsection{Fitting and calibration}

Fit a zero-intercept AR(32) by ordinary least squares on all \(T-32\)
training targets whose 32 preceding observations are also in the training
block:
\[
 (\widehat B_1,\ldots,\widehat B_{32})
 \in\operatorname*{argmin}_{b_1,\ldots,b_{32}}
 \sum_{t=2032}^{1999+T}
 \left(Z_t-\sum_{j=1}^{32}b_jZ_{t-j}\right)^2.
\]
The implementation uses \texttt{numpy.linalg.lstsq} with
\texttt{rcond=None}, with no centering or regularization.
Construct the \(16\times16\) matrices
\[
 (H_0)_{ij}=\widehat B_{i+j-1},\qquad
 (H_1)_{ij}=\widehat B_{i+j},\qquad 1\le i,j\le16.
\]
Retain the leading singular triplet \((u_1,\sigma_1,v_1)\) of \(H_0\),
set \(\mathcal O=\sqrt{\sigma_1}u_1\) and
\(\mathcal R=\sqrt{\sigma_1}v_1^\top\), and compute
\[
 \overline F=\mathcal O^\dagger H_1\mathcal R^\dagger,\qquad
 \overline C=\mathcal O_{1,1},\qquad
 \overline L=\mathcal R_{1,1}.
\]
All three recovered parameters are scalars. The order 32, block size 16,
and rank one are fixed before fitting; there is no hyperparameter search
or stability correction.

On each observed history, the two fitted means are
\[
 \widehat\mu_t^{\rm AR}=\sum_{j=1}^{32}\widehat B_jZ_{t-j},\qquad
 \widehat\mu_t^{\rm SS}=\overline C\overline M_t,\qquad
 \overline M_{t+1}=\overline F\overline M_t+\overline LZ_t,
 \quad\overline M_0=0.
\]
The first 32 AR predictions are set to zero and occur before every
evaluation block. Each model separately estimates its scalar innovation
variance on the calibration block \(\mathcal I\):
\[
 \widehat\Omega
 =\max\left\{10^{-8},\frac1{2000}
 \sum_{t\in\mathcal I}(Z_t-\widehat\mu_t)^2\right\}.
\]
This is the uncentered residual second moment. Fitted parameters and
this variance remain fixed throughout the test block.

The population reference value for the exact state predictor is one.
For AR(32), calculate the optimal residual variance under the
\emph{stationary law}, whose distributions are unchanged by shifting
all time indices. Before observing a finite window, the state variance is
\(P_0=0.18^2/(1-0.98^2)=9/11\). If the current state has conditional
variance \(P_j\), conditioning the Gaussian pair
\((M_{t+1},Z_t)\) gives
\[
 P_{j+1}=\frac{0.8^2P_j}{1+P_j},\qquad
 \Omega_{\rm AR(32)}=1+P_{32}
 \approx1.000000156927612.
\]
The conditional mean is linear in the window, so this is also the
population least-squares residual variance. The completion script
saves this value; its tests independently check it by solving the
population regression using the observation autocovariances.

\subsection{Metrics and the four panels}

For either fitted mean, its test excess mean squared error is
\[
 \operatorname{MSE}_{\rm excess}
 =\frac1{10000}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}(\widehat\mu_t-M_t)^2,
\]
where \(\mathcal T\) is the test block. It measures conditional-mean
error and excludes the irreducible observation variance.
Averaging the Gaussian conditional KL
\(D(\mathcal N(M_t,1),\mathcal N(\widehat\mu_t,\widehat\Omega))\)
over that block gives exactly
\[
 \operatorname{KL}_{\rm step}
 =\frac12\left(\frac{1+\operatorname{MSE}_{\rm excess}}
 {\widehat\Omega}-1+\log\widehat\Omega\right).
\]
The table reports arithmetic means of these metrics over all 50 seeds
at each training size, together with the mean calibration estimate
\(\widehat\Omega\). The AR generator is classified as unstable when
its companion matrix has spectral radius at least one. For the state
generator the criterion is
\(\lvert\overline F+\overline L\overline C\rvert\ge1\);
\(\lvert\overline F\rvert\) is recorded separately for prediction.
Every fit is retained in the summaries.

\Cref{fig:l5-synthetic} is constructed as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item Plot \(M_t\), \(\widehat\mu_t^{\rm AR}\), and
\(\widehat\mu_t^{\rm SS}\) for the first 160 test steps of the
preselected seed 0, \(T=1024\) run: absolute indices 5024 through 5183,
relabeled 1 through 160. These are conditional predictions on a common
observed history, rather than freely generated trajectories.
\item Plot each fitted mean minus \(M_t\) on the same segment.
\item For the same fitted models, plot lags \(j=1,\ldots,64\):
the true \(0.18(0.8)^{j-1}\), the fitted AR coefficients (zero for
\(j>32\)), and \(\overline C\overline F^{j-1}\overline L\).
\item Plot one point per trial, with AR excess MSE horizontally and
state-predictor excess MSE vertically, on logarithmic axes. There are
150 points, distinguished by training size. The diagonal marks equal
errors; crosses identify unstable state generators.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Completion experiments}
\label{app:completion-experiments}

The script \path{scripts/lecture05_completions.py} reads the saved fitted
parameters and covariance estimates and generates
\cref{fig:l5-completion-fit,fig:l5-completion-memory}.
The plotting script uses the resulting saved JSON without refitting
or drawing new randomness.

\paragraph{The fitted models.}
Use seed 0 and \(T=1024\), as in \cref{fig:l5-synthetic}. The common
prompt is the complete prefix \(Z_0,\ldots,Z_{5023}\): warmup, training,
and calibration observations. Run the true and fitted state predictors
through this prefix from their zero initial states; initialize the AR
model with its final 32 observations. Keep all fitted parameters and
calibration variances fixed. Generate three 300-step continuations
using the \(3\times300\) array of standard normal draws from NumPy's
\texttt{default\_rng(20260916)}. Every model uses the same row of this
array for the corresponding path, scaled by its own innovation standard
deviation. Subsequent state updates use the model's generated observations.

The prediction bands are computed analytically. For a scalar innovations
model with parameters \(a,c,\ell,\omega\) and predictive state \(r\)
after the prompt, number future observations by \(h=1,2,\ldots\).
Under its conditional continuation law,
\[
 \E[\widetilde Z_h]=ca^{h-1}r,\qquad
 \operatorname{Var}(\widetilde Z_h)
 =\omega\left(1+(c\ell)^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-2}a^{2j}\right),
\]
with an empty sum at \(h=1\). For an AR model with coefficients \(b_j\),
define its response to one unit of innovation by
\[
 \psi_0=1,\qquad
 \psi_k=\sum_{j=1}^{\min(k,p)}b_j\psi_{k-j}.
\]
Its future mean follows the AR recurrence with future innovations set to
zero, and its variance at step \(h\) is
\(\omega\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\psi_k^2\).
Each band is the conditional mean plus or minus
\(1.959963984540054\) conditional standard deviations.
These are pointwise prediction intervals given the original prompt.

\paragraph{The constant hidden state.}
The reference model is \(U\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\),
\(Z_t=U+W_t\), with independent iid \(W_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\).
Prescribe the prompt \(z_i=2+\xi_i\), \(1\le i\le1000\), using
standard normal draws from \texttt{default\_rng(0)}.
The level 2 is fixed by the experimental design; each model's continuation
law is conditioned on this supplied prompt. The full-history posterior
has \(m_n\approx1.9500\), \(v_n=1/1001\).
Every future observation has conditional mean \(m_n\) and variance
\(1+v_n\) given this prompt.

For coupling with AR innovations, generate the full-history continuation
by its exact sequential Gaussian predictor. Starting from \((m,v)=(m_n,v_n)\),
at each step draw
\[
 z=m+\sqrt{1+v}\,\xi,\qquad
 m_{\rm next}=m+\frac{v}{1+v}(z-m),\qquad
 v_{\rm next}=\frac{v}{1+v}.
\]
This has the same joint continuation law as drawing \(U\) once from its
posterior and then adding independent observation noises.
The fixed-window model is the AR(10) recurrence in
\cref{sec:completion-experiments}, with every coefficient \(1/11\)
and innovation variance \(12/11\). Both use the same \(3\times400\)
array of standard normal draws from \texttt{default\_rng(20260917)}.
Their bands are obtained from the exact conditional variances above.
All parameters are known in this comparison; neither model is fitted
to this one prompt.
The full-history state and true-process panels use the same conditional
law and the same noise draws, so their means, bands, and paths agree
exactly. Both figures order the panels as conditional means, true-process
continuations, AR continuations, and state-model continuations.
